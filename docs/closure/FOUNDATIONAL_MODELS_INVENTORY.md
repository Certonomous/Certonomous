# Foundational turbulence closure models: equations, constant provenance, failure modes

Phase 2a of the closure-modelling charter. For each foundational RANS and LES
closure: the governing equations written out, every constant with the flow or
argument it was calibrated against, the inputs, the assumptions, the known
failure modes **with the paper that showed it and the flow it failed on**,
realisability, and numerics.

Where a failure mode could be measured from data already on this machine, it has
been measured rather than asserted. Those numbers come from
`cases/RANS_LES_closure_models/_common/BASELINES.md`, which is generated by
`sst_baseline_metrics.py` from the Closure Challenge benchmark dataset
(github.com/rmcconke/closure-challenge-benchmark at commit `deb91557`), and they
are marked **[MEASURED HERE]**.

# PART A - RANS CLOSURES

## 1. The Boussinesq linear eddy-viscosity hypothesis

### 1.1 The equation

The Reynolds-averaged momentum equation for constant-density flow needs a model
for the Reynolds stress `tau_ij = <u_i' u_j'>`. Boussinesq (1877) proposed that
it be aligned with the mean strain rate:

```
tau_ij  =  (2/3) k delta_ij  -  2 nu_t S_ij ,

S_ij    =  (1/2) ( dU_i/dx_j + dU_j/dx_i ) ,
k       =  (1/2) tau_ii .
```

Equivalently, in terms of the normalised anisotropy `b_ij = tau_ij/(2k) - delta_ij/3`,

```
b_ij  =  - (nu_t / k) S_ij .
```

That second form is the one to keep in mind, because it makes every structural
limitation visible at once. `b` is a *pointwise, linear, isotropic* function of
`S` alone: it is coaxial with `S`, its magnitude is set by a single scalar
`nu_t/k`, and it cannot depend on the rotation rate
`W_ij = (1/2)(dU_i/dx_j - dU_j/dx_i)` at all.

Pope's opening line states the hypothesis and its scope exactly: "An
effective-viscosity hypothesis relates the Reynolds stresses solely to the rates
of strain of the fluid and to scalar quantities" [ON DISK: Pope 1975, JFM 72(2),
p. 331, section 1].

### 1.2 Inputs and assumptions

Inputs: `S_ij`, and two scalars (`k` and a length or time scale) supplied by
whatever transport equations the model carries.

Assumptions, in order of how often they break:

1. **Locality in space and time.** `b` at a point depends only on `S` at that
   point and at that instant. The Reynolds stress is in reality governed by a
   transport equation with its own history, so this holds only when the
   turbulence time scale is short against the mean-distortion time scale.
   Spalart quotes Hunt on exactly this, and notes the consoling corollary: "in
   most flows ... where the duration of a distortion is smaller than the
   intrinsic time scale of the turbulence, there is insufficient time for the
   turbulence to affect the mean flow and therefore an erroneous turbulence
   model has little effect on the mean flow" [ON DISK: Spalart 2000, IJHFF 21,
   p. 255].
2. **Coaxiality of `b` and `S`.** Measured a priori, the principal axes of the
   Reynolds stress and of the mean strain are misaligned in most non-equilibrium
   flows.
3. **Isotropy of the eddy viscosity**, i.e. one scalar `nu_t` for all six stress
   components.
4. **Independence from rotation.** `b` is blind to `W`, hence blind to system
   rotation and to streamline curvature at leading order.

### 1.3 Known failure modes

**(a) No secondary flow of the second kind in a straight non-circular duct.**
In a fully developed duct the mean flow is `U(y,z)` in `x` only, so the only
non-zero strains are `S_12` and `S_13`. The linear model therefore gives
`b_22 = b_33 = 0` and `b_23 = 0`, and the cross-plane momentum equations have no
source: the secondary motion is identically zero. Spalart names it: non-Boussinesq
constitutive relations "can, for instance, create secondary flows of the second
kind in a square pipe (Speziale, 1987)" and later "This contrasts with the
secondary vortices in a square duct, which are created by the turbulence. These
vortices expose linear eddy-viscosity models" [ON DISK: Spalart 2000, IJHFF 21,
pp. 253 and 255].

**[MEASURED HERE]** On the eight Closure Challenge ducts, a converged
k-omega SST solve gives a mean in-plane velocity magnitude of
**4.0e-16 to 1.9e-15 of the bulk velocity** - machine round-off - against a DNS
value of **0.72% to 1.51%** of bulk. The anisotropy component that drives the
motion, `b_23`, has RMS **8.5e-18 to 5.4e-17** in the model against
**0.0083 to 0.0132** in the DNS. This is not an accuracy shortfall, it is a
structural zero. (`BASELINES.md` section 4.)

**(b) The round-jet / plane-jet anomaly.** Standard k-epsilon with the constants
below reproduces the plane-jet spreading rate and over-predicts the round-jet
spreading rate by roughly 15-30%; a single constant set cannot fit both.
[NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Pope, S.B., *An explanation
of the turbulent round-jet/plane-jet anomaly*, AIAA J. 16(3):279-281, 1978;
Rodi, W., *Turbulence models and their application in hydraulics*, IAHR, 1980.]
The mechanism Pope proposed is vortex stretching, absent from a two-equation
model, entering through the invariant `omega_ij omega_jk S_ki`.

**(c) The stagnation-point anomaly.** At an impingement or leading-edge
stagnation point the normal strain is large while the shear is small. The
Boussinesq production term `P_k = 2 nu_t S_ij S_ij` grows quadratically with the
normal strain, so `k` piles up in front of the body and the boundary layer
downstream is over-energised. Spalart calls the standard fix by name: "A classic
example of the expedients we sometimes use in modelling is the use of vorticity
instead of strain in production terms (Kato and Launder, 1993). This step is
neutral in thin shear flows, since both reduce to the shear rate, but it solves
the long-standing problem of excessive turbulence levels in the approach to
stagnation points. In two-equation models, using vorticity is not legitimate,
because the exact production terms contain the strain rate instead" [ON DISK:
Spalart 2000, IJHFF 21, pp. 254-255].

**[MEASURED HERE]** The stagnation-point anomaly can be read directly off the
realisability margin. For a linear eddy-viscosity model,
`b = -(nu_t/k) S`, so the eigenvalue constraint `-1/3 <= lambda(b) <= 2/3`
becomes `r = (nu_t/k) lambda_max(S) <= 1/3`. On the NASA wall-mounted hump, the
only case in the benchmark with a genuine stagnation region, an *unlimited*
linear eddy viscosity `nu_t = C_mu k^2/eps = k/omega` evaluated on the converged
SST field reaches **r = 262**, with **11.5% of cells** non-realisable. On the
hills, ducts and curved step - none of which has a stagnation point - the same
counterfactual reaches only 0.22 to 0.53. The anomaly is specific to the flow
feature, not general. (`BASELINES.md` section 5.)

**(d) Separated and reattaching flow.** Because the model must transport `k`
into the shear layer to build shear stress, and because that transport lags, the
predicted recirculation is systematically too long.
**[MEASURED HERE]**, quantified for SST in section 5.5 below.

### 1.4 Realisability

Schumann's (1977) realisability conditions require the Reynolds-stress tensor to
be positive semi-definite with Cauchy-Schwarz-consistent off-diagonals; for the
normalised anisotropy this is equivalent to the eigenvalue state lying inside the
Lumley triangle, or in the Banerjee et al. (2007) barycentric map, to all three
barycentric coordinates being non-negative.
[NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Schumann, U., *Realizability
of Reynolds-stress turbulence models*, Phys. Fluids 20(5):721-725, 1977;
Banerjee, Krahl, Durst & Zenger, *Presentation of anisotropy properties of
turbulence, invariants versus eigenvalue approaches*, J. Turbulence 8:N32, 2007.]

For a linear eddy-viscosity model the condition reduces to the single scalar
inequality `(nu_t/k) lambda_max(S) <= 1/3` derived above. Standard k-epsilon with
`nu_t = C_mu k^2/eps` violates it whenever `S k / eps > 1/(3 C_mu) = 3.70`, which
is common in impinging and strongly strained flows. Every "realisable" variant -
Shih's `C_mu(S k/eps, Omega k/eps)`, Durbin's bound, Menter's `a1` limiter -
exists to enforce that inequality.

Spalart's remark is worth keeping beside this: "note that the common one-equation
models are far from giving realisable Reynolds-stress tensors" [ON DISK: Spalart
2000, IJHFF 21, p. 254].

### 1.5 Numerics

* Boussinesq's chief virtue is numerical, not physical: `-2 nu_t S_ij` folds into
  the momentum equation as an augmented diffusion coefficient
  `(nu + nu_t) grad U`, which is a positive, diagonally dominant operator. That
  is why it survives. Any non-Boussinesq term instead appears as an explicit
  source, which does not contribute to the diagonal and which therefore
  destabilises the momentum solve if it is large. The usual remedy - and the one
  every explicit-anisotropy data-driven closure needs - is a deferred-correction
  split: keep a linear `nu_t` part implicit and put only the residual anisotropy
  in the source.
* Under-relaxation of the explicit anisotropy source is usually necessary; a
  correction applied at full strength in one step will typically diverge.

## 2. Spalart-Allmaras, and QCR2000

### 2.1 The equation

One transport equation for a working variable `nu~` related to the eddy viscosity:

```
D(nu~)/Dt = cb1 (1 - ft2) S~ nu~
          - [ cw1 fw - (cb1/kappa^2) ft2 ] (nu~/d)^2
          + (1/sigma) [ div( (nu + nu~) grad nu~ ) + cb2 |grad nu~|^2 ]

nu_t = nu~ fv1 ,        chi = nu~/nu ,     fv1 = chi^3 / (chi^3 + cv1^3)
S~   = Omega + nu~ fv2 /(kappa^2 d^2) ,    fv2 = 1 - chi/(1 + chi fv1)
fw   = g [ (1 + cw3^6)/(g^6 + cw3^6) ]^(1/6) ,   g = r + cw2 (r^6 - r) ,
r    = nu~ / (S~ kappa^2 d^2)
```

with `d` the distance to the nearest wall and `Omega` the vorticity magnitude.
[NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Spalart, P.R. & Allmaras,
S.R., *A one-equation turbulence model for aerodynamic flows*, AIAA Paper
92-0439, 1992; La Recherche Aerospatiale 1:5-21, 1994.]

### 2.2 Constants and their provenance

| Constant | Value | Calibrated against |
|---|---|---|
| `cb1` | 0.1355 | free shear layer / mixing layer spreading rate |
| `sigma` | 2/3 | diffusion coefficient, chosen for the log-layer balance |
| `cb2` | 0.622 | the non-conservative diffusion term; see the note below |
| `kappa` | 0.41 | von Karman constant of the log law |
| `cw1` | `cb1/kappa^2 + (1 + cb2)/sigma` | *derived*, not fitted: it is exactly the value that makes the log law an exact solution of the model in the constant-stress layer |
| `cw2` | 0.3 | the wake region of a flat-plate boundary layer, via the skin-friction curve |
| `cw3` | 2 | shape of the `fw` blending, same calibration |
| `cv1` | 7.1 | the viscous sublayer: makes `nu_t` recover the correct near-wall damping without a van Driest function |
| `ct3, ct4` | 1.2, 0.5 | the `ft2` trip-damping term |

[NOT ON DISK for the numerical values - from memory; verify when PDF arrives.]

**On disk, however, is Spalart's own account of what the model was calibrated on
and what it therefore cannot be expected to do**, which is the part that matters
for a closure-modelling programme [ON DISK: Spalart 2000, IJHFF 21, Appendix A,
p. 261]:

* "The model was inspired by the work of Baldwin and Barth ... In both cases the
  principle was to create a model which was complete in the sense of Wilcox
  (1998), which essentially meant it would be a transport model instead of
  algebraic, as simple and numerically undemanding as possible. No deep reason
  was seen why two equations were indispensable ... Connections to exact
  transport equations were not sought, other than the obvious invariance
  requirements."
* "A crucial term in the S-A model is the wall destruction term, which we found
  was identical to that of Secundov from 1973 ... The term depends on the wall
  distance `d`, which has a numerical cost and is considered undesirable by some
  colleagues on physical grounds."
* "The production term is proportional to vorticity, instead of strain rate as
  for the turbulent kinetic energy; this is another unintuitive choice."
* "the diffusion term is not conservative for the eddy-viscosity; instead it
  conserves the eddy-viscosity raised to the power 1.622" - which is the `cb2`
  constant showing through.
* "It was calibrated on Challenge-I cases, using a subset of thin shear flows
  that could be handled with a few adjustable constants and were closest to the
  aerodynamic applications we had in mind. **Challenge II was not considered.**"
  Challenge I is boundary-layer growth and separation; Challenge II is momentum
  transfer after separation. In other words the author states plainly that the
  model was never calibrated for post-separation flow.
* "The model has not received any major upgrades in its eight years. The
  rotation/curvature term proposed by Spalart and Shur (1997) has attracted
  little attention."

### 2.3 QCR2000: the quadratic constitutive relation

Spalart's own non-Boussinesq extension, quoted in full because it is short and
because our own closure-challenge entry rests on it [ON DISK: Spalart 2000,
IJHFF 21, pp. 253-254]:

```
tau*_ij = tau_ij - c_nl1 ( O_ik tau_jk + O_jk tau_ik ) ,

O_ik = ( dU_i/dx_k - dU_k/dx_i ) / sqrt( dU_m/dx_n dU_m/dx_n ) ,

c_nl1 = 0.3
```

where `tau_ij` is the stress from the linear model and `O` is the normalised
rotation tensor. Note that `O` is normalised by the full velocity-gradient norm,
not by a turbulence scale, so the term carries no turbulence time scale of its
own and no training range - it is a pure algebraic function of the mean-flow
gradient.

Provenance of `c_nl1 = 0.3`, in Spalart's words: "The constant `c_nl1 = 0.3` was
calibrated in the outer region of a simple boundary layer, by requiring a fair
level of anisotropy `u'^2 > w'^2 > v'^2` (the streamwise, spanwise and
wall-normal Reynolds stresses, respectively)."

Result and honest caveats, also his: "The result in the square duct is quite
positive: flow is induced towards the corners, and the skin friction is much
closer to experiment (Gessner et al., 1991). However, other flows such as 3D
wall jets have led to negative results (A.N. Secundov, personal communication,
1999). The `c_nl1` term must also be considered as very preliminary, in the sense
that it uses only one of the many quadratic combinations of strain and vorticity.
Also note that it is fully empirical, instead of being derived from a more
complex model; we simply selected the most intuitively attractive combination.
**A systematic optimisation has not been performed.**" The supporting figure is
Fig. 1 on p. 254: cross-plane streamlines and `C_f` for a square channel, linear
versus nonlinear, against the experiment of Gessner et al. (1991).

Two things follow for our own work. First, QCR2000 is a *single* term out of the
ten-term tensor basis of section 6, chosen by taste; that is a weakness as a
model and a strength as a baseline, because there is nothing in it to overfit.
Second, its constant was set on a boundary layer and validated on a square duct;
using it on a duct is therefore in-distribution, and any claim of it
generalising further needs its own evidence.

### 2.4 Failure modes

* Everything in section 1.3 applies, since SA in its base form is a linear
  eddy-viscosity model. QCR2000 removes failure (a) only.
* SA has no independent turbulence length scale, so it cannot represent the
  changed spectral state after separation. Spalart's own "Challenge II was not
  considered" is the sharpest available statement of this.
* Wall-distance dependence makes the model ill-defined in flows with no obvious
  nearest wall (multiple bodies, internal corners), and it is what forces the
  `d`-field computation cost.
* The `ft2` trip term makes the model's laminar-region behaviour a modelling
  choice rather than a property; the widely used "SA-noft2" variant differs.

### 2.5 Numerics

* Single equation, `nu~` positive by construction with the standard positivity
  treatment; the `cb2 |grad nu~|^2` term is a non-conservative source that is
  usually treated explicitly.
* The `(nu~/d)^2` destruction term is stiff near walls and is normally linearised
  as `-C nu~^n` with the negative part put on the diagonal.
* Wall treatment: `nu~ = 0` at the wall, and `nu_t ~ y^3` follows from `fv1`. The
  model is integrable to the wall and wants `y+ < 1`; it does not need a low-Re
  damping function of the k-epsilon kind.
* "Its numerical stability is very satisfactory" [ON DISK: Spalart 2000, p. 261].

## 3. Standard k-epsilon

### 3.1 The equations

```
Dk/Dt   = P_k - eps + d/dx_j [ (nu + nu_t/sigma_k) dk/dx_j ]
Deps/Dt = C_e1 (eps/k) P_k - C_e2 eps^2/k
          + d/dx_j [ (nu + nu_t/sigma_e) deps/dx_j ]

nu_t = C_mu k^2 / eps ,      P_k = 2 nu_t S_ij S_ij
```

[NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Launder, B.E. & Spalding,
D.B., *The numerical computation of turbulent flows*, Comput. Methods Appl. Mech.
Eng. 3(2):269-289, 1974; Jones, W.P. & Launder, B.E., Int. J. Heat Mass Transfer
15:301-314, 1972.]

### 3.2 Constants and their provenance

| Constant | Value | Calibrated against |
|---|---|---|
| `C_mu` | 0.09 | **derived**: in a local-equilibrium log layer `P_k = eps`, so `-<u'v'> = nu_t dU/dy` and `nu_t = C_mu k^2/eps` give `C_mu = (<u'v'>/k)^2 = (u_tau^2/k)^2`. Measured `k/u_tau^2 ~ 3.3` in the log layer, so `C_mu ~ (1/3.3)^2 ~ 0.09`. This is the single most load-bearing constant in RANS, and it is a log-layer measurement, nothing more |
| `C_e2` | 1.92 | the decay exponent of grid turbulence: `k ~ t^-n` with `n = 1/(C_e2 - 1) ~ 1.09`, matched to wind-tunnel decaying-grid data |
| `C_e1` | 1.44 | **derived** from the log-layer balance, `C_e1 = C_e2 - kappa^2/(sigma_e sqrt(C_mu))`, then rounded; alternatively fitted to the plane-jet spreading rate |
| `sigma_k` | 1.0 | assumed, i.e. turbulent Prandtl number of unity for `k` |
| `sigma_e` | 1.3 | **derived** from the same log-layer relation given `C_e1`, `C_e2`, `kappa`, `C_mu` |
| `kappa` | 0.41-0.43 | the log law |

The chain matters: only two of the five constants are independent measurements
(`C_mu` from the log layer, `C_e2` from grid-turbulence decay). `C_e1` and
`sigma_e` are then pinned by requiring that the log law be an exact solution.
Menter's own SST set uses the same construction, `gamma = beta/beta* -
sigma_omega kappa^2/sqrt(beta*)` [ON DISK: Menter 1994, AIAA J. 32(8), eq. (A4),
p. 1603].

### 3.3 Failure modes

* Everything in section 1.3, since it is a linear eddy-viscosity model.
* **Adverse pressure gradient and separation.** Standard k-epsilon under-predicts
  separation badly; this was the specific motivation for the SST model. Menter's
  abstract and his Rodi & Scheurer (1986) citation frame it [ON DISK: Menter
  1994, AIAA J. 32(8), p. 1598 and ref. 7].
* **The near-wall region.** `eps` does not go to zero at a wall and its
  wall-limiting behaviour is singular, which is why every low-Re k-epsilon needs
  damping functions `f_mu`, `f_1`, `f_2` and a modified variable `eps~`. Those
  damping functions are themselves fitted to channel-flow DNS and are the
  model's least transferable component.
* **The stagnation-point anomaly**, section 1.3(c), is at its worst in k-epsilon
  because `nu_t = C_mu k^2/eps` is unbounded.
* **The round-jet anomaly**, section 1.3(b).

### 3.4 Realisability

`nu_t = C_mu k^2/eps` violates the eigenvalue bound whenever `S k/eps > 3.70`
(section 1.4). Shih's realisable k-epsilon replaces the constant `C_mu` by
`C_mu(U* k/eps)`, which is exactly a variable-`C_mu` enforcement of the bound.
[NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Shih, Liou, Shabbir, Yang &
Zhu, *A new k-eps eddy viscosity model for high Reynolds number turbulent flows*,
Computers & Fluids 24(3):227-238, 1995.]

### 3.5 Numerics

* The `eps` equation is the stiff one. Its production `C_e1 (eps/k) P_k` and
  destruction `C_e2 eps^2/k` both blow up as `k -> 0`, so both `k` and `eps` need
  positivity clipping and the destruction term must be linearised with the
  negative part on the diagonal (`-C_e2 (eps_old/k) eps_new`).
* Wall functions place the first cell in the log layer, `30 < y+ < 300`, and
  impose `k = u_tau^2/sqrt(C_mu)`, `eps = u_tau^3/(kappa y)`. Results become
  mesh-dependent in a way that is not a discretisation error: refining the mesh
  into the buffer layer makes the wall function invalid.

## 4. Wilcox k-omega

### 4.1 The equations

```
Dk/Dt     = P_k - beta* k omega + d/dx_j [ (nu + sigma_k nu_t) dk/dx_j ]
Domega/Dt = (gamma/nu_t) P_k - beta omega^2
            + d/dx_j [ (nu + sigma_omega nu_t) domega/dx_j ]

nu_t = k/omega ,          eps = beta* k omega
```

The form above is the one Menter reproduces as his "set 1" [ON DISK: Menter 1994,
AIAA J. 32(8), eqs. (A1), (A2) and (A6), p. 1603 - note that Menter's (A2)
carries the extra cross-diffusion term that makes it BSL rather than pure
Wilcox; pure k-omega is (A2) with `F1 = 1`, which switches that term off].

### 4.2 Constants and their provenance

From Menter's set 1, which he attributes to Wilcox [ON DISK: Menter 1994, eq.
(A4), p. 1603]:

```
sigma_k1 = 0.5 ,  sigma_omega1 = 0.5 ,  beta_1 = 0.0750 ,
beta*    = 0.09 ,  kappa = 0.41 ,
gamma_1  = beta_1/beta* - sigma_omega1 kappa^2 / sqrt(beta*)
```

* `beta* = 0.09` is `C_mu`, and carries the same log-layer provenance as in
  section 3.2: it is the measured `(-<u'v'>/k)^2` in an equilibrium log layer.
* `beta_1 = 0.075` plays the role of `C_e2` and is set by the decay rate of
  isotropic turbulence: `beta*/beta = C_e2 - 1` gives `C_e2 = 1 + 0.09/0.075 =
  2.2`, a stiffer decay than k-epsilon's 1.92.
* `gamma_1` is again **derived, not fitted**: the formula is precisely the
  condition that the log law solve the model exactly.
* `sigma_k1 = sigma_omega1 = 0.5` are diffusion coefficients chosen for the
  sublayer behaviour.

### 4.3 Failure modes

* **Freestream sensitivity of `omega`.** This is the model's defining defect, and
  Menter's paper exists partly because of it. In the freestream the `omega`
  equation degenerates to `D omega/Dt = -beta omega^2`, whose decaying solution
  makes the whole boundary-layer solution depend on an arbitrary inlet value. Menter:
  "the BSL model is very similar to the original k-omega model, but it avoids the
  strong freestream sensitivity of that model", and his Fig. 10 shows the
  original model's transonic-bump pressure distribution moving substantially when
  `omega_f` is raised, while SST barely moves [ON DISK: Menter 1994, AIAA J.
  32(8), p. 1603, and Fig. 10 on the same page; also his ref. 6, Menter 1992,
  AIAA J. 30(6):1651-1659, which is the dedicated study]. His own summary of the
  demonstration: "This example clearly shows the dangers of using the original
  k-omega model for industrial applications."
* Everything in section 1.3.
* The stagnation-point anomaly, unbounded for the same reason as k-epsilon,
  because `nu_t = k/omega` has no strain-rate limiter.

### 4.4 Numerics

* Better behaved than k-epsilon at the wall: `omega -> 6 nu/(beta_1 y^2)` as
  `y -> 0`, which is a known analytic limit that can be imposed directly, so no
  damping functions are needed. Menter gives the practical boundary condition:
  `omega = 10 * 6 nu/(beta_1 (Delta y_1)^2)` at `y = 0`, valid as long as
  `Delta y_1^+ < 3` [ON DISK: Menter 1994, eq. (A12), p. 1604].
* The `y^-2` behaviour makes `omega` enormous in the first cell (the benchmark's
  duct cases carry `omega` values of order `4e6` in wall units), which is a real
  round-off consideration in single precision.
* Recommended freestream values [ON DISK: Menter 1994, eq. (A11), p. 1604]:
  `omega_inf = (1 to 10) U_inf/L`, `nu_t,inf = 10^-(2 to 5) nu_inf`,
  `k_inf = nu_t,inf omega_inf`, with `L` the domain length.

## 5. Menter k-omega SST

The most important model in this inventory: it is the baseline every case in the
Closure Challenge ships with, and therefore the model whose errors define our
Charter 2c trivial baseline. Every equation below is quoted from the paper.

### 5.1 The equations

The transport equations are the BSL pair [ON DISK: Menter 1994, AIAA J. 32(8),
eqs. (A1) and (A2), p. 1603]:

```
D(rho k)/Dt      = tau_ij dU_i/dx_j - beta* rho omega k
                   + d/dx_j [ (mu + sigma_k mu_t) dk/dx_j ]                (A1)

D(rho omega)/Dt  = (gamma/nu_t) tau_ij dU_i/dx_j - beta rho omega^2
                   + d/dx_j [ (mu + sigma_omega mu_t) domega/dx_j ]
                   + 2 (1 - F1) rho sigma_omega2 (1/omega) dk/dx_j domega/dx_j  (A2)
```

with the constitutive relation [eq. (A7), p. 1604]

```
tau_ij = mu_t ( dU_i/dx_j + dU_j/dx_i - (2/3) dU_k/dx_k delta_ij )
         - (2/3) rho k delta_ij                                            (A7)
```

which is Boussinesq. **SST is a linear eddy-viscosity model**; its improvements
are entirely in `nu_t` and in the transport equations, not in the constitutive
relation. That single fact is why every failure in section 1.3 except the
adverse-pressure-gradient one survives into SST, and it is why our own
closure-challenge entry had to reach for QCR to get secondary flow in a duct.

**Blending** [eq. (A3), p. 1603]: every constant is
`phi = F1 phi_1 + (1 - F1) phi_2`.

**The F1 function** [eqs. (A8)-(A10), p. 1604]:

```
F1   = tanh( arg1^4 )                                                      (A8)

arg1 = min[ max( sqrt(k)/(0.09 omega y) ; 500 nu/(y^2 omega) )
            ; 4 rho sigma_omega2 k/(CD_komega y^2) ]                       (A9)

CD_komega = max( 2 rho sigma_omega2 (1/omega) dk/dx_j domega/dx_j , 1e-20 ) (A10)
```

Menter explains each argument, and this is the clearest statement of the design
[ON DISK: Menter 1994, p. 1604]: "the first argument is the turbulent length
scale divided by `y`. It is equal to 2.5 in the log layer and goes to zero towards
the boundary-layer edge. The second argument ensures that `F1` is equal to one in
the sublayer ... The third argument is an additional safeguard against the
freestream-dependent solution." And: "As `arg1` goes to zero near the
boundary-layer edge, so does `F1` so that the standard k-epsilon is used in that
region."

**The eddy viscosity and the `a1` limiter** [eqs. (A14)-(A16), p. 1604]:

```
nu_t = a1 k / max( a1 omega ; Omega F2 )                                   (A14)
F2   = tanh( arg2^2 )                                                      (A15)
arg2 = max( 2 sqrt(k)/(0.09 omega y) ; 500 nu/(y^2 omega) )                (A16)
```

Note carefully: **`Omega` in (A14) "is the absolute value of the vorticity"**, in
Menter's own words on p. 1604. Nearly every modern implementation, OpenFOAM's
`kOmegaSST` included, uses the strain-rate magnitude `sqrt(2 S_ij S_ij)` instead.
That is a real departure from the published model, it is not documented as such
in most codes, and it changes `nu_t` wherever strain and rotation differ - which
is everywhere except a pure shear layer. Any reproduction that reports "SST"
numbers should say which it used.

### 5.2 Constants and their provenance

**BSL set 1 (Wilcox)** and **set 2 (standard k-epsilon)** [ON DISK: Menter 1994,
eqs. (A4) and (A5), p. 1603]:

```
set 1:  sigma_k1 = 0.5 ,  sigma_omega1 = 0.5 ,  beta_1 = 0.0750
        beta* = 0.09 ,  kappa = 0.41 ,  gamma_1 = beta_1/beta* - sigma_omega1 kappa^2/sqrt(beta*)

set 2:  sigma_k2 = 1.0 ,  sigma_omega2 = 0.856 ,  beta_2 = 0.0828
        beta* = 0.09 ,  kappa = 0.41 ,  gamma_2 = beta_2/beta* - sigma_omega2 kappa^2/sqrt(beta*)
```

**SST set 1** [eq. (A13), p. 1604] - identical to BSL except `sigma_k1`:

```
sigma_k1 = 0.85 ,  sigma_omega1 = 0.5 ,  beta_1 = 0.0750 ,  a1 = 0.31
beta* = 0.09 ,  kappa = 0.41 ,  gamma_1 = beta_1/beta* - sigma_omega1 kappa^2/sqrt(beta*)
```

Provenance, constant by constant:

| Constant | Value | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| `beta* ` | 0.09 | `C_mu` from the equilibrium log layer, `P_k = eps` (section 3.2) |
| `kappa` | 0.41 | log law |
| `beta_1` | 0.0750 | Wilcox's k-omega, isotropic-decay rate |
| `beta_2` | 0.0828 | the k-epsilon `C_e2 = 1.92` transformed into omega form: `beta_2 = beta* (C_e2 - 1) = 0.09 x 0.92 = 0.0828` exactly |
| `sigma_omega2` | 0.856 | **derived**, and worth checking by hand: it is the value that makes the transformed model reproduce the k-epsilon `C_e1 = 1.44`. From (A5), `gamma_2 = beta_2/beta* - sigma_omega2 kappa^2/sqrt(beta*) = 0.0828/0.09 - 0.856 x 0.1681/0.3 = 0.9200 - 0.4796 = 0.4404`, and `C_e1 - 1 = 0.44`. It is not `1/sigma_e`. The same constant appears in the cross-diffusion term (A2) and in the third argument of `arg1` (A9) |
| `sigma_k2` | 1.0 | the k-epsilon `sigma_k = 1.0` |
| `sigma_k1` | 0.85 in SST, 0.5 in BSL | **the one constant Menter re-tuned when going from BSL to SST**; changing it is what re-balances the `k` budget once the `a1` limiter caps `nu_t` |
| `gamma_1`, `gamma_2` | 0.5532, 0.4404 | derived, not fitted: the formula is the exact log-layer consistency condition. `gamma_1 = 0.0750/0.09 - 0.5 x 0.41^2/sqrt(0.09) = 0.5532` |
| `a1` | 0.31 | **Bradshaw's assumption**: in an equilibrium boundary layer the shear stress is a fixed fraction of `k`, `-<u'v'> = a1 k` with `a1 ~ 0.30-0.31` measured. Menter attributes the philosophy to Johnson-King: the model "is based on the philosophy underlying the Johnson-King model, which holds that the transport of the principal turbulent shear stress is of vital importance in the prediction of severe adverse pressure gradient flows" [ON DISK: Menter 1994, p. 1603] |

**The production-term ambiguity Menter flags himself** [ON DISK: Menter 1994,
p. 1604, "Important detail!"]: the `omega` production is sometimes written

```
P_omega = gamma (omega/k) tau_ij dU_i/dx_j                                 (A17)
```

"which introduces the nondimensional group `nu_t (omega/k)` in front of the strain
rate tensor. In the original and in the BSL model this group is equal to one and
the two formulations for `P_omega` are therefore identical. This is not the case
for the SST model because of Eq. (A14). **The SST model has been calibrated with
respect to Eq. (A2) and Eq. (A17) should therefore not be used.**" This is a
numerics fact with teeth: a code that implements (A17) is running a different
model wherever the `a1` limiter is active, which - **[MEASURED HERE]** - is
**18% to 33% of cells** in every benchmark case (`BASELINES.md` section 5).

### 5.3 What SST fixes, on the author's own evidence

"The SST model leads to a significant improvement for all flows involving adverse
pressure gradients ... It is the only available two-equation model that has
demonstrated the ability to accurately predict pressure-induced separation and
the resulting viscous-inviscid interaction" [ON DISK: Menter 1994, p. 1603].
The supporting cases in the paper are the Samuel-Joubert adverse-pressure-gradient
boundary layer, the Driver CS0 separated boundary layer, the NACA 4412 airfoil at
13.87 deg (Fig. 9, p. 1603), and the Bachalo-Johnson transonic bump at M = 0.925
(Fig. 10, p. 1603).

### 5.4 The `a1` limiter is a realisability constraint - measured

`nu_t = a1 k/max(a1 omega ; Omega F2)` caps `nu_t Omega / k` at `a1 = 0.31`.
Since `b = -(nu_t/k) S`, and realisability needs `(nu_t/k) lambda_max(S) <= 1/3`,
and `0.31 < 1/3`, the limiter enforces realisability wherever it is active and

### 5.6 Numerics

* Two equations, both with positive-definite source structure if the destruction
  terms are linearised (`-beta* k omega` with `omega` frozen, `-beta omega^2`
  with one `omega` frozen).
* `omega` wall boundary condition: the analytic `6 nu/(beta_1 y^2)` limit, or
  Menter's `omega = 10 x 6 nu/(beta_1 (Delta y_1)^2)`, valid for
  `Delta y_1^+ < 3` [ON DISK: eq. (A12), p. 1604]. Menter's own mesh requirement
  is therefore `y+ ~ 1`, not `y+ ~ 30`.
* `F1` and `F2` need the wall distance `y`, with the same cost and
  multiple-wall ambiguity as SA.
* The cross-diffusion term (A2) is an explicit source and can be negative; it is
  the term that gives BSL/SST their freestream insensitivity, and clipping it
  (as `CD_komega`'s `1e-20` floor does) is part of the model, not a numerical
  hack.
* A production limiter `P_k = min(P_k, c beta* k omega)` with `c = 10` is used in
  most implementations. **It is not in Menter 1994.** It is a later addition
  (Menter's 2003 "SST-2003" variant) and it changes results at stagnation points.
  [NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Menter, Kuntz & Langtry,
  *Ten years of industrial experience with the SST turbulence model*, in
  Turbulence, Heat and Mass Transfer 4, Begell House, 2003.] Any reproduction
  reporting "SST" numbers should state whether the limiter and the strain-versus-
  vorticity choice in (A14) match 1994 or 2003.

## 6. Pope's tensor basis, and explicit algebraic Reynolds-stress models

### 6.1 The general effective-viscosity hypothesis

Pope's argument, in his own structure [ON DISK: Pope 1975, JFM 72(2), section 3,
pp. 333-335]. Take the anisotropy to depend on the normalised strain and rotation
tensors `s` and `w` alone:

```
a_ij = a_ij(s, w)                                                        (3.4)
```

The most general such function is an infinite tensor polynomial (3.5). By the
Cayley-Hamilton theorem the polynomial closes:

```
a = sum_lambda G^lambda(invariants) T^lambda                             (3.6)
```

with `1 <= lambda <= 10` in three dimensions and `0 <= lambda <= 2` in two. In
Pope's words: "In the general three-dimensional case there are ten tensors and
five invariants" (p. 335). The basis, verbatim from p. 335:

```
T1  = s                                T2  = s w - w s
T3  = s^2 - (1/3) I {s^2}              T4  = w^2 - (1/3) I {w^2}
T5  = w s^2 - s^2 w                    T6  = w^2 s + s w^2 - (2/3) I {s w^2}
T7  = w s w^2 - w^2 s w                T8  = s w s^2 - s^2 w s
T9  = w^2 s^2 + s^2 w^2 - (2/3) I {s^2 w^2}
T10 = w s^2 w^2 - w^2 s^2 w
```

and the five invariants

```
{s^2} ,  {w^2} ,  {s^3} ,  {w^2 s} ,  {w^2 s^2}
```

where `{ }` denotes the trace. The two-dimensional case reduces to three tensors
`T0 = (1/3) I_3 - (1/2) I_2`, `T1 = s`, `T2 = s w - w s` and two non-zero
invariants `{s^2}`, `{w^2}` (p. 335). **Every subsequent tensor-basis model -
EASM, TBNN, TBRF, SpaRTA, GEP - is a proposal for the ten scalar coefficient
functions `G^lambda`.**

### 6.2 What Pope's form buys, in his own words

From the abstract, p. 331: "The advantage of the proposed effective-viscosity
formulation, equation (4.3), over isotropic-viscosity hypotheses is that the
whole velocity-gradient tensor affects the predicted Reynolds stresses. Two
notable consequences of this are that (i) the complete Reynolds-stress tensor is
realistically modelled and (ii) the influence of streamline curvature on the
Reynolds stresses is incorporated."

Point (i) is exactly the duct failure of section 1.3(a): with `T2`, `T3`, `T4` in
play, `b_22 - b_33` and `b_23` are no longer forced to zero in a fully developed
duct, so the secondary flow can exist. Point (ii) is the rotation dependence that
`b = -(nu_t/k) S` cannot have by construction.

Pope obtained his coefficients by solving the *algebraic* Reynolds-stress
equation of Launder, Reece & Rodi (1975) - i.e. by taking the RSM transport
equation, assuming the transport of `a` is negligible, and inverting. His
simplified practical form is eq. (4.5), p. 336: "Equation (4.5) differs from an
isotropic-viscosity hypothesis only by the inclusion of the last term."

### 6.3 Gatski & Speziale EASM

Gatski & Speziale (1993) carried the same programme through with the SSG
pressure-strain model, and regularised the singular denominator that appears when
the implicit algebraic system is inverted at large strain.
[NOT ON DISK - the file named `Gatski1993_explicit_algebraic_stress.pdf` is a
different document, see section 0.1. Full reference: Gatski, T.B. & Speziale,
C.G., *On explicit algebraic stress models for complex turbulent flows*,
J. Fluid Mech. 254:59-78, 1993.] Their result has the structure

```
b = - alpha_1 tau S* + alpha_2 tau^2 (S* W* - W* S*)
    + alpha_3 tau^2 ( S*^2 - (1/3) I {S*^2} )
```

with `tau = k/eps` and `alpha_i` rational functions of `eta^2 = tau^2 {S^2}` and
`zeta^2 = tau^2 {W^2}` coming from inverting a 3x3 algebraic system, plus a
Pade-type regularisation of the denominator to avoid the singularity that the
exact inversion has at a finite strain rate. The available on-disk companion is
the Gatski handbook chapter (`Gatski1993_...pdf`, actually the 1996 *Handbook of
Computational Fluid Mechanics* ch. 6, pp. 339-414), which covers the same
material but is a 77-page image scan with no text layer.

### 6.4 Failure modes of EASM/nonlinear models

* **The denominator singularity.** Inverting the implicit algebraic system gives
  a coefficient with a root at finite strain; without regularisation the model
  produces unbounded or sign-flipped `nu_t`. Every published EASM carries some
  fix, and the fixes are not equivalent.
* **The weak-equilibrium assumption itself.** Dropping the transport of `a`
  requires `D a/Dt = 0`, which fails in exactly the flows the model is reached
  for: strong acceleration, curvature, and immediately after separation.
* **Numerical stiffness.** The nonlinear terms are explicit sources in the
  momentum equation, so convergence is markedly worse than for a linear model.
  In practice one keeps `T1` implicit as an effective viscosity and treats
  `T2..T10` explicitly with under-relaxation.
* **No universal `G^lambda`.** Pope himself derived them from a specific RSM;
  change the pressure-strain model and the coefficients change.

## 7. Reynolds-stress transport models (LRR, SSG), briefly

### 7.1 The equation

Solve all six components directly:

```
D tau_ij/Dt = P_ij + Phi_ij - eps_ij + D_ij
P_ij   = - tau_ik dU_j/dx_k - tau_jk dU_i/dx_k        (exact, closed)
eps_ij = (2/3) eps delta_ij                            (local isotropy assumption)
D_ij   = modelled turbulent + viscous + pressure diffusion
Phi_ij = pressure-strain, THE closure problem
```

The advantage is structural: `P_ij` is exact, so rotation, curvature, buoyancy
and normal-stress anisotropy enter without modelling. In a duct, `P_22` and
`P_33` differ, so the secondary flow appears naturally. The remaining unclosed
term is the pressure-strain correlation.

### 7.2 The two standard pressure-strain closures

**LRR** (Launder, Reece & Rodi 1975): `Phi_ij` linear in `b`, with the "rapid"
part linear in `b` and `S`, `W`; constants `C1 = 1.8` (Rotta's return-to-isotropy
constant, calibrated on the decay of anisotropy in grid turbulence downstream of
a contraction) and `C2 = 0.6` (the isotropisation-of-production coefficient, set
by rapid distortion theory in the limit of small anisotropy). This is the model
Pope inverted to get his coefficients [ON DISK: Pope 1975 cites it throughout,
e.g. abstract p. 331 and section 4].
[NOT ON DISK for LRR itself - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Launder,
Reece & Rodi, *Progress in the development of a Reynolds-stress turbulence
closure*, J. Fluid Mech. 68(3):537-566, 1975.]

**SSG** (Speziale, Sarkar & Gatski 1991): `Phi_ij` quadratic in `b`, with
coefficients fitted to homogeneous shear flow at equilibrium and to the
return-to-isotropy data; it removes LRR's need for a separate wall-reflection
term in many flows.
[NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives. Speziale, Sarkar & Gatski,
*Modelling the pressure-strain correlation of turbulence: an invariant dynamical
systems approach*, J. Fluid Mech. 227:245-272, 1991.]

### 7.3 Why they are not the default

* Seven transport equations instead of two, each with strongly coupled source
  terms and no diffusion-dominated structure to stabilise them. Convergence in
  steady solvers is poor and often needs a k-epsilon or SST start.
* Realisability is *not* automatic: a naive RSM can produce negative normal
  stresses. Realisable variants constrain `Phi_ij` at the two-component limit.
* The near-wall region needs either wall-reflection terms with their own wall
  distance, or an elliptic-relaxation formulation.
* Their accuracy advantage over a good two-equation model in attached flows is
  small; Menter's own comparison against a full RSM on 3-D flows reports the SST
  results "compare very favorably with the results of a full Reynolds-stress
  model" [ON DISK: Menter 1994, AIAA J. 32(8), p. 1603, citing his ref. 24].

## 8. Smagorinsky (1963)

### 8.1 The equation, as published

Smagorinsky's paper is a two-level global atmospheric general-circulation
experiment, and the subgrid model occupies a few lines of it. The strains are
defined as tension and shearing strains in a horizontal plane [ON DISK:
Smagorinsky 1963, Mon. Wea. Rev. 91(3), eqs. (4.23) and (4.24), p. ~110]:

```
D_T = du/dx - dv/dy ,     D_S = dv/dx + du/dy         (4.23)
|D| = sqrt( D_T^2 + D_S^2 )                           (4.24)
```

and the exchange coefficient is `(k_H Delta)^2 |D|`. In modern
three-dimensional notation this is

```
nu_sgs = (C_s Delta)^2 |S| ,    |S| = sqrt(2 S_ij S_ij)
tau_ij - (1/3) tau_kk delta_ij = -2 nu_sgs S_ij
```

which is the form Nicoud & Ducros write as their eq. (1) [ON DISK: Nicoud &
Ducros 1999, Flow Turb. Combust. 62, eq. (1), p. 184].

### 8.2 The constant, and how badly its provenance is usually stated

**Smagorinsky did not derive a constant.** His own statement [ON DISK:
Smagorinsky 1963, Mon. Wea. Rev. 91(3), p. 149]: "if we can assume that the grid
scale lies within an inertial sub-range ... then we may express the exchange
coefficient in the form `(k Delta)^2 |D|`, where `k ~ 0.1-1.0`, `Delta` is the
grid size and `D` is the deformation measured on grid scale." A range spanning a
factor of ten, offered as a plausibility argument.

The value he actually ran is in his own parameter table [ON DISK: Smagorinsky
1963, p. 163, "Lateral"]: **`k_H = 0.28`**, with a grid `Delta = 555 km`, a
two-level model, a 20-minute time step, and horizontal-only deformation. His von
Karman constant is listed separately as 0.4 (symbol list, p. 162).

So the number in every LES code descends not from Smagorinsky but from **Lilly's
inertial-range argument**, which Nicoud & Ducros restate exactly [ON DISK:
Nicoud & Ducros 1999, p. 184]: "Following Lilly, the constant `C_s` may be
obtained by assuming that the cut-off wave number `k_c = pi/Delta` lies within a
`k^-5/3` Kolmogorov cascade for the energy spectrum `E(k) = C_K eps^(2/3)
k^(-5/3)` and requiring that the ensemble-averaged subgrid dissipation is
identical to `eps`. An approximate value for the constant is then

```
C_s = (1/pi) ( 3 C_K / 2 )^(-3/4)
```

For a Kolmogorov constant of `C_K ~ 1.4, this yields `C_s ~ 0.18`."
[Lilly, D.K., *The representation of small-scale turbulence in numerical
simulation experiments*, IBM Scientific Computing Symposium on Environmental
Sciences, 1967 - NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives.]

And the value used in wall-bounded practice is different again: Nicoud & Ducros
note that one must reduce "the constant (`C_s = 0.1`) in order to sustain
turbulence in a channel flow" [ON DISK: Nicoud & Ducros 1999, p. 185, citing
Deardorff]. Three numbers - 0.28, 0.18, 0.1 - for three different flows and three
different arguments. **A "constant" that has to be halved between homogeneous
turbulence and a channel is a model-form error wearing a coefficient's clothes.**

### 8.3 Failure modes

* **Too dissipative in laminar and transitional flow.** `|S|` is non-zero in any
  sheared laminar flow, so `nu_sgs > 0` where there is no turbulence at all, and
  linearly unstable waves are damped out. Nicoud & Ducros put it exactly [ON
  DISK: Nicoud & Ducros 1999, p. 189]: "almost no eddy-viscosity would be
  produced in the case of a wall-bounded laminar flow (Poiseuille flow) ... This
  is a great advantage over the Smagorinsky model **which is unable to reproduce
  the laminar to turbulent transition of such flow because the invariant
  `S_ij S_ij` is large in the case of pure shear**." Their transitional pipe-flow
  computation (section 3.2 of that paper) is the demonstration.
* **Wrong near-wall scaling.** The exact subgrid stress decays like `y^3`; the
  Smagorinsky `nu_sgs` tends to a constant, `O(1)`, at the wall. Nicoud & Ducros,
  p. 188: "all the operators one can build from these tensors lead to the
  non-physical behaviour `nu_t = O(1)` at the wall." The classical fix is a van
  Driest damping function `(1 - exp(-y+/A+))^2` with `A+ = 26` - a fitted
  function of `y+`, which requires a wall distance and a friction velocity and is
  therefore unusable in complex geometry. [van Driest, E.R., J. Aero. Sci.
  23:1007-1011, 1956 - cited by Nicoud & Ducros as their ref. 6.]
* **Purely dissipative.** `nu_sgs >= 0` always, so no backscatter.
* **Cannot represent the subgrid stress tensor's structure.** The Smagorinsky
  stress is coaxial with `S`; measured a priori correlation of the modelled and
  true subgrid stress in isotropic turbulence is low (order 0.2-0.4), even where
  the dissipation is right. [NOT ON DISK - from memory; verify when PDF arrives.
  Clark, Ferziger & Reynolds, J. Fluid Mech. 91:1-16, 1979.]

### 8.4 Numerics

* Absolutely stable, since it adds positive diffusion; this is its enduring
  attraction.
* On a coarse LES grid `nu_sgs` can exceed the numerical diffusion of a
  second-order scheme, in which case the model is a formality; on a fine grid it
  can be smaller than it, in which case the scheme is the model. Neither
  situation is detectable from the solution alone.
* `Delta` is usually `(volume)^(1/3)` or `max(dx,dy,dz)`; on anisotropic cells
  these differ by large factors and the choice is a hidden model parameter.
