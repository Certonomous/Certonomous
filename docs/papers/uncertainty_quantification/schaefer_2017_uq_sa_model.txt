The goal of this work is to quantify the uncertainty and sensitivity of the SpalartAllmaras turbulence model in Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes codes due to uncertainty
in the values of model coefficients for three wall-bounded flows, and to rank the contribution
of each coefficient to uncertainty in various output flow quantities of interest. Specifically,
uncertainty quantification of turbulence model coefficients is performed for a subsonic flat
plate, the RAE 2822 transonic airfoil, and transonic flow over the NASA Common Research
Model. The Boeing-developed BCFD code is used as the flow solver. The uncertainty
quantification analysis employs stochastic expansions based on non-intrusive polynomial
chaos for efficient uncertainty propagation. Integrated force coefficients, loads reports,
pressure coefficient distributions, and skin friction coefficient distributions are considered
as uncertain output quantities of interest. Turbulence model coefficients are treated as
epistemic uncertain variables represented with intervals. Sobol indices are used to rank
the relative contributions of each coefficient to the total uncertainty in the output quantities
of interest. A new method of characterizing the Spalart-Allmaras coefficient uncertainties
is discussed and comparisons are made with the old method. The new method employs
relations between coefficients which were fundamental to the original development of the
model, and in particular allows only a much narrower range for the variations of the
logarithmic law.

I.

Introduction

Due to the highly complex and chaotic nature of turbulence, the current physical understanding is
incomplete (and possibly unknowable). Turbulence models have been created to predict turbulent flows using
computational fluid dynamics (CFD) at high Reynolds number; however, the lack of physical knowledge has
forced modelers to use dimensional analysis and a large amount of judgement to identify several constants
which close or tune the model equations (e.g., choice of transport variables, number of equations, etc.).
Numerical values of the coefficients in current turbulence models are chosen using a combination of heuristic
and empirical decision making. While modelers typically use experimental data to validate their choice of
model coefficients, a turbulence model is not guaranteed to be universally valid for an arbitrary flow.
The goal of this work is to quantify the uncertainty and sensitivity of transonic, wall-bounded flows due
to epistemic uncertainties in the values of the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) turbulence model coefficients. In a
previous study performed by Schaefer et al.,1 the authors showed that only a small subset of the SA model
coefficients is responsible for the majority of the uncertainty in two 2D, transonic, wall-bounded flows. These
results were later shown to be independent of mesh and flow solver in studies by Schaefer2 and Schaefer et

In the previous studies by Schaefer et al.,1–3 the authors treated all coefficients in the SA model as
independently uncertain. This method of characterizing input uncertainties for the SA model results in
large uncertainties for some output quantities of interest (QoI) for the flows considered in the work. The
determination was made that relations exist (and should be enforced) between certain coefficients within the
SA model to maintain appropriate behavior for canonical flows. This work includes uncertainty quantification
(UQ) results using these relations.
To minimize the large computational expense associated with UQ of high-fidelity CFD simulations with
traditional sampling methods (e.g., Monte Carlo), stochastic expansions based on non-intrusive polynomial
chaos (NIPC) are used as an efficient means of uncertainty propagation in this work.5 All model coefficients
are treated as epistemic uncertain variables (i.e., uncertainty due to lack of knowledge and not due to
inherent, systemic uncertainty). Sobol indices,6 which are global nonlinear sensitivity indices based on
variance decomposition, are used to rank the relative contributions of each coefficient to the total uncertainty
in the QoI. For the CFD problems considered in this work, QoIs include integrated force coefficients, running
loads, pressure coefficient distibutions, and skin friction coefficient distributions.
NIPC is not the only UQ method applicable to CFD simulations. In a recent study by Barth,7 quadrature
methods are employed to characterize the uncertainties in the lift, drag, and pressure coefficients for several
wall-bounded flows. Each analysis includes either freestream, geometric, or turbulence model uncertainties.
In contrast to the current study, the study by Barth treats all input uncertainties as aleatory (i.e., characterized by probability density functions), not epistemic. The advantages and disadvantages of this approach
are discussed by Barth.7
UQ of CFD simulations with aleatory input uncertainties has also been performed using Bayesian approaches. Edeling et al.8 describe a stochastic error estimate of turbulence models based on variability in
model coefficients. Using Bayesian model-scenario averaging, a stochastic estimate of the output quantities
of interest in unmeasured (prediction) scenarios is obtained. Papadimitriou and Papadimitriou9 employ a
Bayesian inference framework and analytical approximations to quantify the uncertainty of the SA model
due to uncertainty in its coefficients for a backwards-facing step problem.
Section II contains details about the flow solver and CFD problems employed in this work. The SpalartAllmaras turbulence model is discussed in Section III. An overview of the uncertainty quantification approach
is given in Section IV, and all results are presented in Section V. Finally in Section VI, conclusions are made
and suggestions for future work are presented.

II.

Flow Solver and CFD Problem Overviews

The Boeing-developed BCFD10 code was chosen as the flow solver for this work. The details of BCFD
are provided in Section II.A. The subsonic flat plate problem is described in Section II.B, followed by the
RAE 2822 airfoil in Section II.C. Information for the Common Research Model CFD problem is provided
in Section II.D.
II.A.

Flow Solver: BCFD

The flow solver used in this work is BCFD version 8.0, discussed in Ref. 10. BCFD is an unstructured, grid
cell-centered, three-dimensional, finite-volume RANS code capable of solving steady and unsteady Euler,
laminar, or turbulent flows. The code allows changes to the values of the SA turbulence model coefficients
to support the uncertainty analyses. Multiple cases from the NASA Turbulence Model Resource (TMR)
webpage11 have been used to validate the SA implementation. For all three CFD problems in this work, the
UQ training cases were restarted from converged solutions using the baseline SA coefficients for improved
convergence time.

IV.

Uncertainty Quantification Approach

Uncertainties in computational models fall into two categories: aleatory and epistemic. Aleatory uncertainties represent inherent variations in a system, whereas epistemic uncertainties arise due to lack of
knowledge. (For example, the outcome of a coin flip is aleatory because it is due to inherent chance; however
if a coin is placed on a table and covered up, the uncertainty in heads/tails is epistemic because it is due to
a lack of knowledge.) In this work, all SA model coefficients are treated as epistemic uncertain variables due
to the lack of a complete physical understanding of turbulence. Section IV.A describes the point-collocation
non-intrusive polynomial chaos method employed to characterize this uncertainty. Sobol indices are used as
a sensitivity measure, and in Section IV.B a description and derivation of Sobol indices is presented.

An oversampling ratio of 1.0 yields the minimum number of samples required to produce a response
surface. Hosder et al.22 demonstrated that an oversampling ratio of np = 2.0 gives a better approximation
to the statistics at each polynomial degree they considered. For this reason, np = 2.0 is used for all of
the UQ analyses in this work except for the flat plate analysis with three uncertain coefficients (the flat
plate solutions are computationally inexpensive to calculate, so in that instance oversampling was raised to
np = 3.0). Given np > 1.0, Eq. (13) is overdetermined and can be solved using a least squares approach.
In the current work, a polynomial order of two (p = 2) is used for all UQ analyses except for the CRM
analysis with all nine SA coefficients treated as independently uncertain; that analysis uses p = 3. The
results of the third order CRM analysis are shown to be convergent (as np increases) and indistinguishable
from second order CRM results in Section V; these observations justify the use of p = 2 for all other analyses,
as the CRM is by far the most complicated flow studied in this work. Furthermore, the choice of p = 2
has been validated and used successfully in previous studies of complex stochastic CFD problems involving
hypersonic flows.23,24

V.A.

Flat Plate

The subsonic flow over a flat plate is well-documented.27,28 For this reason, there is very little expected
uncertainty associated with the QoI, such as skin friction coefficient (Cf ). The flat plate flow is included in
this work as a way to test whether or not the characterization of uncertainty for the SA model is physically
reasonable; a physically reasonable characterization of SA model coefficient uncertainties should produce flat
plate Cf profiles with little uncertainty (i.e., small epistemic intervals) because the true flat plate Cf profiles
are well-known and were used to determine appropriate model coefficient values.
Plots of Cf versus Reynolds number based on x (Rex ) were generated for all of the cases used in the
flat plate UQ analyses. Using NIPC, response surfaces for Cf were generated at each x-location along the
plate. Sobol indices for Cf were also calculated at each x-location. The Cf profiles for the IUCA are
included in Fig. 4a. The plot contains data for the baseline case, UQ training cases, and minimimum and
maximum Cf bounds calculated from the NIPC response surfaces. Fig. 4b shows the corresponding Sobol
index distributiion for Cf of each SA model coefficient. Recall that coefficients with large Sobol indices
contribute more significantly to uncertainty than coefficients with small Sobol indices. Fig. 4b allows for
the detailed identification of where along the plate each SA model coefficient contributes significantly to
uncertainty in Cf . For clarity, only the coefficients with the most significant contributions are shown in
Fig. 4b.
Note that the minimum and maximum Cf curves may not in general correspond to one flow solution
each because each point along each curve is calculated from the response surface generated by using the Cf
values of the UQ training cases at that specific x-location. While there exist flow solutions that pass through
or very near to the minimum and maximum Cf curves at each point, there is no flow solution guaranteed
to pass through all of the minimum or all of the maximum points.
Skin friction results were also generated for the flat plate RDA. These results are shown in Fig. 5. The
epitemic interval for Cf is much smaller in Fig. 5a than in Fig. 4a. This observation is due to the the use of
the σ and κ relations in Eqns. (7) - (10); with this approach, the RDA is more closely following the original
development and tuning of the SA model, which included matching flat plate skin friction and boundary
layer profiles. The reduced epistemic interval for Cf also indicates that the characterization of uncertainty
in the RDA may be more appropriate than the IUCA characterization for future analyses. In both the IUCA
and RDA for the flat plate, κ is the dominant source of uncertainty in Cf , followed by σ (Figs. 4b and 5b).
This is remarkable, considering that κ is a measured quantity and a foundation of turbulence knowledge
(although that has been challenged in some circles), which is not the case for empirical parameters unique
to the SA model such as σ.

NASA Common Research Model

One challenge associated with the NIPC approach is to determine the appropriate number of samples (or
equivalently oversampling ratio) required to obtain an accurate response surface. As Ns and np grow,
rows are added to α∗ (x, ξ) and Ψi (ξ) in Eqn. (13) and the values of the NIPC response surface coefficients
(α0 , α1 , ..., αP ) can change. The change in response surface coefficients is reflected in changes to the variance
decomposition of Sobol indices (see Eqns. (14) - (20)), making Sobol indices good metrics of statistical
convergence as Ns and np increase. Sobol indices also capture non-linear effects, enabling them to be used
to evaluate the choice of NIPC polynomial order.
The Sobol index convergence for CL and CD of the CRM with all nine SA coefficients is shown in Fig. 8.
Convergence is plotted for both a polynomial order of p = 2 and p = 3. All four plots show that the Sobol
indices converge very quickly with np ; this behavior is due to the fact that several SA coefficients do not
contribute significantly to the uncertainty in CL and CD . The inclusion of the insignificant coefficients in
the NIPC analysis necessitates additional sampling which provides more information for the calculation of
response surface coefficients related to significant SA coefficients. The converged values of the Sobol indices
for p = 2 are the same as for p = 3, indicating that third order effects are negligible for this problem. As
discussed in Section IV, this justifies the use of p = 2 for all other UQ analyses in this work.

V.D.

Summmary of Results

When comparing the results of all three CFD problems, some general trends appear. If Eqns. (7) - (10) are
used to calculate cb1 , cb2 , cw2 , and cv1 , the epistemic intervals for all QoIs decrease compared to considering
all coefficients to be independently uncertain. When the relations are not used, the uncertainty in Cf for the
flat plate is large, indicating that the SA model is not properly calibrated for certain combinations of model
coefficients. If Eqns. (7) - (10) are employed, the UQ process more closely follows the original development
methodology of the SA model, and more believable epistemic intervals are obtained. For each CFD problem,
κ is the dominant source of uncertainty when the relations are enforced.
Both the RAE 2822 and CRM feature regions where the flow is likely to be retarded or separated. In
these regions, σ is the largest contributor to uncertainty in the QoIs. In regions of attached flow, κ is the
largest contributor to uncertainty. Although σ dominates the uncertainty in retarded and separated regions,
its effect is not influential globally (i.e., on integrated force coefficients) because the majority of the flow for
the RAE 2822 and CRM is attached. This is especially apparent for the CRM, which has a much larger
portion of its wetted area strongly attached compared to the RAE 2822. The trends in σ and κ sensitivities
arise because σ is relevant to non-equilibrium boundary layers, while κ drives log-law effects.
In the IUCA for the CRM, κ and σ were found to be the most significant sources of uncertainty, followed
by smaller contributions from cb1 , cv1 , and cw2 . This result confirms the findings by Schaefer et al.1–3 for a
complex three-dimensional flow.

VI.

Conclusions and Future Work

Several analyses were performed to quantify the uncertainty in the Spalart-Allmaras turbulence model
due to uncertainty in model coefficients. The CFD problems considered include a flat plate, RAE 2822
transonic airfoil, and the NASA Common Research Model. The uncertainty propagation employed stochastic
expansions based on non-intrusive polynomial chaos. Sobol indices were used both as global non-linear
sensitivity metrics and as a means to track convergence of the non-intrusive polynomial chaos response
surfaces. Output quantities of interest included integrated force coefficients, as well as distributed quantities
such as skin friction coefficient, pressure coefficient, and running loads. A new method of characterizing
the input uncertainties for the Spalart-Allmaras model was developed, which led to more realistic flat plate
solutions and reduced epistemic intervals for all output quantities of interest.
The independently uncertain coefficient analysis for the NASA Common Research Model in this work
confirmed the previous two-dimensional results by Schaefer et al.1–3 for a complex three-dimensional flow:
when the Spalart-Allmaras turbulence model is used for transonic, wall-bounded flows, κ and σ are responsible for the majority of the uncertainty in output flow quantities of interest, independent of grid resolution,
grid topology, flow solver, and geometric dimensionality. In both the independently uncertain and reduced
dimensionality analyses, common trends emerged for regions in which different coefficients in the SpalartAllmaras turbulence model have the largest impact on skin friction and pressure coefficient uncertainties.

In all cases studied, uncertainty in κ is the most significant contributor to uncertainty in output quantities
of interest in regions where the boundary layer is developed with a significant log layer. Uncertainty in
σ becomes the dominant contributor to uncertainty in the outputs when the boundary layer is less welldeveloped, such as in regions immediately post-shock, near separated flow, and in the presence of strong
vortices.
The authors recommend that future uncertainty analysis associated with turbulence models more closely
follow the reduced dimensionality analysis approach of identifying the model designer intent to define relationships among the closure coefficients rather than treating them as independent. While considering each
coefficient as independent can provide insight to the sensitivities, it can also lead to excessive estimates of
uncertainty. Future work will include an uncertainty quantification analysis associated with the original
foundational flow fields used to obtain Eqns. (7) - (10) to more completely represent the uncertainty in
the inputs to the model development. Uncertainty analysis of other turbulence models, such as the Wilcox
(2006) k-ω model or Menter Shear Stress Transport model, would be insightful to determine if the observations associated with sensitivity of parameters in the Spalart-Allmaras model are general trends. To provide
a comparison of the significance of the turbulence model uncertainties, an analysis will also be performed
to assess the uncertainty and sensitivity of the output quantities of interest due to other sources of input
uncertainty, including the freestream Mach number, angle of attack, and angle of sideslip. While this comparison may vary from cases to case, performing a mixed uncertainty analysis including multiple sources of
uncertainty will help to identify the level of interconnection among the different sources of uncertainty.
