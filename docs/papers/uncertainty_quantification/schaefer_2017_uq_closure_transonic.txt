The goal of this work is to quantify the uncertainty and sensitivity of commonly used turbulence models in
Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes codes due to uncertainty in the values of closure coefficients for transonic wallbounded flows and to rank the contribution of each coefficient to uncertainty in various output flow quantities of
interest. Specifically, uncertainty quantification of turbulence model closure coefficients is performed for transonic
flow over an axisymmetric bump and the RAE 2822 transonic airfoil. Three turbulence models are considered: the
Spalart–Allmaras model, Wilcox (2006) k-ω model, and Menter shear-stress transport model. The FUN3D code
developed by NASA Langley Research Center is used as the flow solver. The uncertainty quantification analysis
employs stochastic expansions based on non-intrusive polynomial chaos for efficient uncertainty propagation. Several
integrated and point quantities are considered as uncertain outputs for both computational fluid dynamics problems.
Closure coefficients are treated as epistemic uncertain variables represented with intervals. Sobol indices are used to
rank the relative contributions of each closure coefficient to the total uncertainty in the output quantities of interest.
This study identifies a number of closure coefficients for each turbulence model for which more information will
reduce the amount of uncertainty in the output significantly for transonic wall-bounded flows.

I.

Introduction

Downloaded by MIT Libraries on March 7, 2026 | http://arc.aiaa.org | DOI: 10.2514/1.J054902

T

URBULENCE is one of the greatest unsolved mysteries of
classical physics. Because of the highly complex and chaotic
nature of the phenomenon, the current physical understanding of
turbulence is incomplete. Efforts have been made to create turbulence
models to predict turbulent flows by using computational fluid
dynamics (CFD); however, the lack of physical knowledge has forced
modelers to use dimensional analysis and other methods to identify
several constants that close the model equations (i.e., “closure
coefficients”). The numerical values of the closure coefficients in
current turbulence models are chosen by using a combination of
heuristic and empirical decision making. Although modelers
typically use experimental data to validate their choice of closure
coefficients, a turbulence model is not guaranteed to be universally
valid for any arbitrary flow.
The goal of this work was to quantify the uncertainty and
sensitivity of commonly used turbulence models in Reynoldsaveraged Navier–Stokes (RANS) codes due to uncertainty in the
values of closure coefficients for transonic wall-bounded flows and to
rank the contribution of each coefficient to uncertainty in various
output flow quantities of interest. Specifically, three turbulence
models are considered: the Spalart–Allmaras one-equation model
(SA) [1], 2006 Wilcox k-ω two-equation model (W2006) [2], and
Menter shear-stress transport two-equation model (SST) [3]. Each
model features its own unique set of closure coefficients, which are
discussed further in Sec. IV.
FUN3D, an unstructured RANS code from NASA Langley
Research Center, was chosen as the flow solver for all simulations in
this work. More information about FUN3D is included in Sec. III.A.
Two CFD problems are considered in this work. The first is an
axisymmetric transonic bump validation case from Bachalo and
Johnson [4], which is discussed in detail in Sec. III.B. The second
CFD problem is the RAE 2822 transonic airfoil, which is discussed in
detail in Sec. III.C.
To minimize the large computational expense associated with
uncertainty quantification of high-fidelity CFD simulations with
traditional sampling methods (e.g., Monte Carlo), stochastic
expansions based on non-intrusive polynomial chaos (NIPC) are
used as an efficient means of uncertainty propagation in this work [5].
All closure coefficients were treated as epistemic uncertain variables
(i.e., uncertainty due to lack of knowledge and not due to inherent,
systemic uncertainty). Sobol indices [6], which are global nonlinear
sensitivity indices based on variance decomposition, were used to
rank the relative contributions of each closure coefficient to the total
uncertainty in the output quantities of interest. For the transonic
bump, the output quantities of interest are drag coefficient, pressure
coefficient, skin friction coefficient, and separation bubble size. For
the RAE 2822, the output quantities of interest are lift coefficient,
pressure and skin friction components of drag coefficient, and
pressure coefficient.
Section II contains a literature review of previous research related
to this work. In Sec. III, an overview of the flow solver and CFD test
problems is presented. In Sec. IV, the details of each of the turbulence
models considered here are discussed. A summary of the closure
coefficients and their domains is presented for each model. In Sec. V,
the uncertainty quantification (UQ) analysis and computational
expense is discussed in further detail. In Sec. VI, the results of all UQ
analyses are presented. Finally, in Sec. VII, conclusions are made and
some suggestions for future work are presented.

Godfrey and Cliff [8] employed the sensitivity-equation method to
quantify the sensitivities of the Baldwin–Lomax, Spalart–Allmaras,
and Wilcox k-ω turbulence models due to modeling coefficients;
however, they did not quantify the uncertainty in the results. Hosder
et al. [9] quantified the uncertainty in nozzle efficiency for a transonic
diffuser problem; however, this uncertainty was due to the choice of
pressure ratios, computational grids, flux limiters, and turbulence
models. Han and Hosder [10] performed a mixed uncertainty
quantification of a subsonic two-dimensional (2-D) hump with flow
control, which included a scaling factor on the turbulent eddy viscosity
definition in the Spalart–Allmaras model. Platteeuw et al. [11]
employed probabilistic collocation (based on a chaos expansion) to
quantify the uncertainty in a flat plate case based on uncertainties in the
k-ϵ turbulence model coefficients. They used direct numerical
simulation (DNS) data to obtain input uncertainties, but the DNS data
were not a fundamental part of their statistical method. Dunn et al. [12]
employed a similar approach to obtain input uncertainties, but instead
used the Latin hypercube sampling method for the quantification of
uncertainty. Emory et al. [13] investigated the model-form uncertainty
in the SST model using epistemic uncertainties; in addition to avoiding
the need for prior data, this method avoids the need to assume
probability density functions for the inputs. Dow and Wang [14] solved
an inverse RANS problem with the k-ω model using adjoints.
In a recent work by Edeling et al. [15], the authors state that “The
turbulence closure model is the dominant source of error in most
Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes simulations, yet no reliable
estimators for this error component currently exist.” Furthermore,
“there is no consensus on the best coefficient values for most
[turbulence] models, as evidenced by the wide range of coefficients
seen in the literature.” Many current efforts to address these concerns
employ Bayesian approaches. Edeling et al. [15] describe a stochastic
error estimate of turbulence models based on variability in model
coefficients. Using Bayesian model-scenario averaging, a stochastic
estimate of the output quantities of interest in unmeasured (prediction)
scenarios is obtained. Papadimitriou and Papadimitriou [16] also
performed uncertainty quantification of turbulence modeling using a
Bayesian approach. In their study, Papadimitriou and Papadimitriou
employed Bayesian inference framework and analytical approximations to quantify the uncertainty of the Spalart–Allmaras turbulence
model due to uncertainty in its closure coefficients for a backwardfacing step problem. In another study, Cheung et al. [17] used Bayesian
calibration techniques and showed that cv1 and κ were the most wellinformed coefficients in the Spalart–Allmaras model. Duraisamy et al.
[18] and Parish and Duraisamy [19] employed Bayesian inversion and
machine learning to characterize the uncertainty in turbulence models
due to their functional forms.
It is generally acknowledged that the RANS turbulence models are
imperfect [20], and one alternative approach is to use large-eddy
simulations (LES). Similar to RANS, LES also features uncertainties.
Lucor et al. [21] use generalized polynomial chaos to perform a
sensitivity analysis on the Smagorinsky subgrid-scale stress model with
LES. Meldi et al. [22,23] continued this research by investigating the
effects of uncertainties in the shape of the energy spectrum on the
Smagorinsky constant. Tran et al. [24] described an adaptive hierarchical
sparse grid surrogate modeling approach to Bayesian inference. Using
this method, they performed an uncertainty quantification of LES results
for a subcritical flow around a circular cylinder due to parametric
uncertainties in the Smagorinsky model.

II.

Literature Review

There is currently a high demand for uncertainty quantification and
sensitivity analysis in turbulence modeling. In a coordinated study
between NASA, Boeing, and others, Slotnick et al. [7] propose a
vision for the capabilities of CFD technology in the year 2030, as well
as a number of suggestions for how to reach that point. One of the
authors’ immediate recommendations is to integrate NIPC methods
and other reduced-order model formulations with current CFD
technology. Here we outline some of the previous work on the
sensitivity and uncertainty analysis of turbulence models.

B. Problem 1: Axisymmetric Transonic Bump

The first CFD problem considered in this work is an axisymmetric
transonic bump validation case from Bachalo and Johnson [4]. The
test article in the experiment consisted of a thin-walled cylinder that
extended 61 cm upstream of the bump’s leading edge, and the
cylinder had an outside diameter of 15.2 cm with a bump chord of
20.32 cm. The flow has freestream M  0.875, T  540 ° R, and
Re  2.763 × 106 (based on the bump length of c  1). All flow
solutions were obtained by using the 721 × 321 grid from the
turbulence modeling resource (TMR) website,†† which is the second
finest in a series of uniformly refined grids. The mesh contains
462,882 nodes; 1,155,121 edges; and 230,400 hexahedral cells. It
consists of a 1 deg wedge rotated about the x axis. The sides of the
mesh are treated as periodic interfaces; they are periodic with each
other and rotated through 1 deg to simulate axisymmetry. The surface
of the bump and cylinder has a solid wall boundary condition, and all
other boundary conditions are far-field Riemann. A graphical
overview of the axisymmetric transonic bump problem is included in
Fig. 1. The circles below the mesh in the figure represent the
axisymmetry about the x axis. An example pressure contour plot is
included in Fig. 2 for the transonic bump case obtained with the SA
baseline model. The pressure contours in Fig. 2 are scaled by the
freestream density and speed of sound squared; note that the
freestream nondimensional pressure is equal to the inverse of the ratio
of specific heats.

V.

Uncertainty Quantification Approach

Uncertainties in computational models fall into two categories:
aleatory and epistemic. Aleatory uncertainties represent inherent
variations in a system, whereas epistemic uncertainties arise due to lack
of knowledge. (For example, the outcome of a coin flip is aleatory
because it is due to inherent chance; however, if a coin is placed on a
table and covered up, the uncertainty in heads/tails is epistemic because
it is due to a lack of knowledge.) In this work, all closure coefficients
are treated as epistemic uncertainvariables due to the lack of a complete
physical understanding of turbulence. It should be noted that epistemic
uncertainties are not the same as probabilistic uncertain variables with
uniform distributions, because no probability density function is
assumed by the former. Our approach is to propagate the epistemic
input uncertainty to the output quantities of interest with surrogate
response surfaces and quantify the epistemic uncertainty in the output
by determining the maximum and minimum value of the response
predicted by the surrogate model.
Rather than resorting to Monte Carlo simulations for uncertainty
quantification, stochastic expansions based on point-collocation
NIPC were employed to reduce computational expense [5]. The
strategy of point-collocation NIPC is to create a surrogate model via
least-squares approach (i.e., polynomial response surface) by using
the CFD output obtained at a number of Latin hypercube sample
points for the propagation of uncertainty. NIPC has been used
successfully for CFD applications by West and Hosder [32], Adya
et al. [33], and Hosder et al. [34]. For more examples, see the review
by Najm [35].
An explanation of point-collocation NIPC given by West and
Hosder [32] follows. With the polynomial chaos approach, a
stochastic response function α (e.g., drag coefficient, pressure or
skin friction coefficient at a given point in the flowfield) can be
decomposed into separable deterministic and stochastic components
within a series expansion:

An oversampling ratio of 1.0 yields the minimum number of
samples required to produce a response surface. Hosder et al. [38]
demonstrated that an oversampling ratio of np  2.0 gives a better
approximation to the statistics at each polynomial degree they
considered. For this reason, np  2.0 was used for all of the UQ
analyses in this work. Given np > 1.0, Eq. (36) is overdetermined and
can be solved using a least-squares approach. In the current work, a
polynomial order of two p  2 was used for all UQ analyses. In
Sec. VI, it is shown that the choice of p  2 gives sufficient accuracy in
the representation of the CFD responses by the stochastic surrogate
model. Furthermore, the choice of p  2 has been validated and used
successfully in previous work on complex stochastic CFD problems
involving hypersonic flows [32,39].
Sobol indices (global nonlinear sensitivity indices) were used to
rank the relative contributions of each closure coefficient to the total
uncertainty in the output quantities of interest. Sobol indices can be
derived via Sobol decomposition, which is a variance-based global
sensitivity analysis method. This derivation uses the polynomial
chaos expansion coefficients calculated in Eq. (36) and is included in
Appendix B. All Sobol indices reported in this work are the total
(combined) Sobol indices, which include both the individual and
mixed contributions of each coefficient.
Because of the bounded nature of epistemic input uncertainties,
Legendre polynomials are used in this study as the basis functions.
For the purpose of the calculation of Sobol indices only, each input
parameter can be interpreted as a uniformly distributed probabilistic
parameter because Legendre polynomials are the optimal basis
functions for such variables in the construction of polynomial chaos
expansions. However, although stochastic response surfaces created
with the NIPC approach allow the calculation of confidence intervals
along with other various statistics of the output for probabilistic
(aleatory) input, in our uncertainty quantification approach, only the
maximum and minimum of the response are calculated to determine
the epistemic interval for each uncertain output.
Initially, all of the closure coefficients in Tables 1–3 were used in
the uncertainty quantification analyses. The results of these analyses
are presented in the next section. The closure coefficients that
contribute most to the uncertain outputs in the original analyses are
then used as the uncertain variables in a second set of reduceddimensionality analyses (RDA). It is important to note the distinction
between the accuracy of the surrogate model created as a function of a
determined set of uncertain variables and the accuracy in
approximating the stochastic problem with the created surrogate.

The uncertainty quantification and sensitivity results of the
axisymmetric transonic bump and RAE 2822 airfoil are consistent with
one another. For the W2006 and SST models, every closure coefficient
that is significant to at least one output quantity of interest for the
transonic bump is also significant to at least one output quantity of
interest for the RAE 2822 and vice versa. This agreement is nearly
observed by the SA model as well, with only one coefficient significant
to the transonic bump, but not the RAE 2822, and only two coefficients
significant to the RAE 2822, but not the transonic bump. A summary of
all of the significant closure coefficients is included in Fig. 10.
One interesting result is that κ appears in all three turbulence
models considered in this work, yet it was only found to contribute
significantly to uncertainty for the SA model. This result is likely
due to the fact that the log layer calibration in the SA model is
very dependent on the value of κ (χ  κy ), but the log layer
calibrations of the two-equation
p models contain additional
parameters (γ  β∕β − σ w κ 2 ∕ β ).
Compared with the transonic bump results, the RAE 2822 results
are much less sensitive to changes in all three turbulence models’
closure coefficients; this is likely because the transonic bump features
a separation region and the airfoil does not. Note also that cw3 in the
SA model contributes significantly to uncertainty in the transonic
bump problem, but not the RAE 2822. Figure 7b shows that cw3 is
only significant to uncertainty in Cf in the separation region, and
Table 12 shows that cw3 contributes significantly to uncertainty in
separation bubble size. These observations suggest that cw3 is only
significant to separated flows.
Whether separation is present or not, the same trends apply to all
models for both CFD problems; the SST model is most sensitive to
changes in its closure coefficients, and the SA and W2006 models are
roughly equally sensitive to changes in their closure coefficients.
Uncertainties in Cp are particularly large in the shock region of each
flow for all three turbulence models. The fact that the Sobol index
distributions change significantly with x for both flows suggests that
certain coefficients contribute more significantly to particular flow
features (such as a shock or separation bubble) than others.
There may be two possible reasons that the output quantities for
SST have such large epistemic intervals compared with those of SA
or W2006 (see Figs. 6, 7, and 9 for graphical examples). First,
although verified by expert opinion, the epistemic intervals for some
SST closure coefficients may extend beyond the physically
reasonable range. In this case, the SST model may produce
nonphysical results. A second reason for the SST model’s large
output epistemic intervals could be that the model itself is not as
robust to changes in its coefficients as SA or W2006. Particularly, the
use of blending functions could lead to a highly sensitive model.
Given that SST and W2006 share many of the same coefficients, it
seems that this second reason for the SST model’s sensitivity is more
likely. This conclusion is also consistent with the statement made by
the author of this model (see Sec. IV) that small changes (5–10%) in
the values of the SST closure coefficients can yield large changes in
model behavior.

VII.

Conclusions

Two detailed uncertainty quantification studies focusing on
turbulence model closure coefficients were performed on an
axisymmetric transonic bump at zero degrees angle of attack and the
RAE 2822 transonic airfoil at a lift coefficient of 0.744. The transonic
bump has freestream M  0.875 and Re  2.763 × 106 , and the
RAE 2822 has freestream M  0.729 and Re  6.5 × 106 . The
turbulence models considered were the Spalart–Allmaras model,
Wilcox (2006) k-ω model, and Menter shear-stress transport model.
The uncertainty quantification employed stochastic expansions
based on non-intrusive polynomial chaos as an efficient means of
uncertainty propagation. The drag coefficient, wall pressure and skin
friction coefficient distributions, and shock-induced separation
bubble size were considered as uncertain outputs for the transonic
bump. The lift coefficient, pressure and skin friction components of
drag coefficient, and wall pressure coefficient distribution were
considered as uncertain outputs for the RAE 2822. Sobol indices
were used to rank the relative contributions of each closure
coefficient to the total uncertainty in the output quantities of interest.
This sensitivity information can be used as a potential starting point in
the future advancement of the turbulence models considered here. In
addition, this study provides the parametric uncertainty in the
turbulence models due to lack of knowledge for the closure
coefficient values, which will be a crucial component in the
quantification of model-form uncertainty.
Based on the results, the following conclusions can be made: For
the SA model, the coefficients that contribute most to uncertainty in
the output quantities of interest are σ, κ, cv1 , cb1 , cw2 , ct3 , ct4 , and cw3 .

For the W2006 model, the coefficients that contribute most to
uncertainty in the output quantities of interest are β , Clim , and σ w .
For the SST model, the coefficients that contribute most to
uncertainty in the output quantities of interest are σ w1 , β ∕β1 , β ∕β2 ,
β , and a1 . With the exception of cw3 , ct3 , and ct4 in the SA model, all
other closure coefficients were found to be significant to both the
axisymmetric transonic bump and RAE 2822 transonic airfoil.
In the future refinement of the models discussed here, the authors
suggest that studies should focus on increasing knowledge of
appropriate values for κ and σ for the SA model, Clim and σ w for the
W2006 model, and β for the SST model. Improved knowledge of the
values of these particular closure coefficients will have the largest
impact on reducing the parametric uncertainty in integrated quantities
of interest for the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes simulations of
wall-bounded transonic flows. Future work should also focus on
determining why certain closure coefficients contribute more or less
to uncertainty at different flow locations. For example, research
should be done to determine why σ in the SA model becomes
important to Cf for the transonic bump after the reattachment point,
but not before. The global sensitivity results reported in this paper
will depend on the epistemic intervals chosen for each closure
coefficient (Tables 1–3). If significant changes were made to these
intervals through the acquisition of new knowledge on closure
coefficients, it would be desirable to conduct similar sensitivity
studies on the refined turbulence models in the future. Future
experimental work should report the uncertainties in experimental
data, which would allow for model-form uncertainty quantification
and for determining the optimum values of the closure coefficients for
certain flow types.
Although the focus of this paper is on 2-D transonic wall-bounded
turbulent flows for the uncertainty analysis, the authors believe that
the results and conclusions presented here will be a significant
contribution to the computational fluid dynamics and turbulence
modeling literature. The accurate modeling of transonic wallbounded turbulent flows is important for aerodynamicists due to the
vast number of aircraft configurations that are designed to operate in
this flow regime. By including two test cases relevant to this flow
regime, the authors aim to generalize the findings for this type of flow.
