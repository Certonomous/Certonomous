The University of Manchester, Manchester, M13 9PL, United Kingdom
This study quantifies epistemic uncertainty associated with the coefficients of two closure models used in Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes simulations. The incompressible flow
over a NACA0012 airfoil at angles of attack ranging from 2◦ to 17◦ is simulated using the
Spalart–Allmaras and Menter’s Shear Stress Transport (SST) turbulence models available in
OpenFOAM. For each model, the three most influential coefficients are treated as uncertain
parameters. The quantities of interest are the lift and drag coefficients. Uncertainty propagation
is performed using non-intrusive polynomial chaos expansion (NIPCE), and global sensitivity
analysis is conducted using Sobol indices. The results show that uncertainty increases with
the angle of attack, exhibiting a sharp growth near and after stall. Overall, the Menter’s SST
model displays higher uncertainty than the Spalart–Allmaras model, and the drag coefficient is
more sensitive to model-based uncertainty than the lift coefficient. In the Spalart–Allmaras
model, the von Kármán coefficient and 𝜎 are the most influential parameter before and post
stall, respectively. In contrast, for the Menter’s SST model, the ranking of the most influential
parameters on the resulting uncertainty varies with angle of attack.

I. Introduction
urbulent flows arise in a range of aerospace applications, from flow over wings to flow inside engines, see e.g. [1].
Due to the complex and chaotic nature of turbulent flows, developing a comprehensive physical understanding of
turbulent flows remains challenging. Various modelling approaches in Computational Fluid Dynamics(CFD) have been
proposed to predict turbulent flows [2]. In particular, the Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) approach relies
on on closure models to represent the effects of turbulence on the mean flow. Numerous closure models have been
proposed in the literature, see e.g. [3]. Such models usually contain several parameters or coefficients, the values of
which are assigned based on theoretical analyses, empirical correlations, experimental data, and heuristic judgment.
The applicability of these coefficients may vary across flow regimes, Reynolds numbers, or geometrical configurations.
Consequently, the lack of knowledge regarding the most appropriate coefficient values introduces epistemic uncertainty
into RANS predictions of key quantities of interest (QoIs) [4, 5]. It is, therefore, essential to assess the robustness of the
turbulence closure models with respect to variations in their coefficients, noting the potential impact on the accuracy
and reliability of CFD predictions [5, 6]. This motivates the application of rigorous uncertainty quantification (UQ)
techniques to CFD simulations in aerospace engineering.
This study aims to quantify uncertainty and assess sensitivity in aerodynamic QoIs arising from epistemic
uncertainty—specifically, uncertainty due to lack of knowledge –in the coefficients of RANS turbulence closure models.
We consider two widely used models: the Spalart–Allmaras (SA) model [7] and the Menter’s Shear Stress Transport
(SST) model [8]. For each model, the uncertainty due to the three most influential parameters identified by Schaefer
et al. [5] are considered, see Section III for details. The QoIs examined are the lift and drag coefficients of a NACA0012
airfoil over angles of attack ranging from 0◦ to 17◦ . The flow is assumed to be incompressible and simulated using
OpenFOAM [9], a widely-used finite-volume flow solver.
A significant amount of work has been done on UQ of model coefficients for RANS turbulence models under different
conditions and using different frameworks. Edeling et al. [10], developed a posterior error estimate of turbulence models
using a Bayesian model scenario averaging (BMSA) method. The author, however, concluded that this method was
computationally intensive for complex geometries. In previous studies by Schaefer et al. [5, 6], the authors identified the
most influential model coefficients for various integrated coefficients and flow QoIs for the SA [7], Menter’s SST [8],
and 𝑘 − 𝜔 [11] turbulence models. The study was carried out for the subsonic flat plate, the RAE 2822 airfoil, and the
NASA Common Research Model [12] based on the configuration of the transonic civil aircraft. The studies employed
Point Collocation NIPCE for uncertainty propagation and the Sobol Decomposition for global sensitivity analysis.
These studies were limited to a fixed Lift coefficient (𝐶 𝐿 ) or angle of attack. In another study by Schaefer et al. [13], it
was shown that these results were independent of the flow solver, the mesh topology, and the scaled dimensionality of
the geometry.
In a study by Li et al. [14], Bayesian uncertainty analysis and Bayesian calibration are performed on model
coefficients of Menter’s Shear Stress Model [8] with pressure coefficient as the quantity of interest. The study was
performed on RAE 2822 airfoil and ONERA M6 wing for transonic flow at three different angles of attack. This
study was limited to pressure coefficient as the quantity of interest and limited number of angles of attack. Maruyama
et al. [15], quantified the epistemic uncertainty in Spalart Allmaras [7] turbulence model using a Bayesian framework
for RAE 2822 airfoil and transonic XRF1 aircraft. The authors concluded that the epistemic uncertainties in closure
coefficients result into uncertainties in flow quantities of interest downstream of the shock. Han and Hosder [16],
conducted UQ and sensitivity analysis of Spalart Allmaras [7] turbulence model for synthetic jet actuator. The author
concluded that the reference experimental velocity profiles were enveloped by the confidence interval of the RANS
simulations. In another study by West IV and Hosder [17], the uncertainty propagation in hypersonic re-entry flows
was studied employing NIPCE with sparse sampling. The study concluded that accurate surrogate model could be
constructed with a limited number of samples.
Yang et al. [18], employed Bayesian optimization to optimally calibrate the coefficients of the Menter’s SST
model [8] for wall pressure in supersonic flows. The authors reported a decrease in average relative error through
employing optimal model coefficients. Further, the flow field before and after the calibration was consistent, confirming
the robustness of the model with respect to the variations in its coefficients. This trend is, however, inconsistent
to the study by He et al. [19], which used the metamodel-based Monte Carlo method to investigate the parametric
uncertainty of the Spalart Allmaras [7] model for compressor flow features. The author concluded that recalibration of
the model coefficients to improve the prediction of specific flow features would lead to degraded accuracy in other QoIs,
highlighting the need for modifying the structure of the Spalart Allmaras [7] model. Bin et al. [20], employed Bayesian
optimization to recalibrate the model coefficients and functions of Spalart Allmaras [7] model for an axisymmetric jet,
airfoil near wake, and a fully developed channel flow. The calibrated model showed improvements in both separated

II. Flow Solver and CFD Case
A. Flow Solver
The flow simulations in this study are performed using OpenFOAM v2012 [9]. OpenFOAM is an open-source,
finite-volume–based CFD solver. Steady-state RANS simulations are carried out with the simpleFoam solver. A
second-order upwind scheme is employed for the discretization of the nonlinear convective terms in the momentum
equations, while a central-difference scheme is used for the diffusive terms. To support UQ analysis, an automated
Python workflow was developed to handle case setup, systematic modification of the RANS model coefficients, execution
of the simulations, and post-processing of the results.
B. CFD Case
The incompressible flow over a two-dimensional NACA0012 airfoil is considered at Reynolds number 𝑅𝑒 = 6 × 106 ,
that is based on the inflow velocity, airfoil chord length, and fluid viscosity. The details of this set-up along with the
reference experimental data are taken from the NASA Turbulence Modeling Resource, "2D NACA0012 Validation
Case" [21]. The chord length of the airfoil is 𝑐 and the computational domain considered for meshing is 10𝑐 to neutralize
any blockage affects. A structured C-mesh consisting of of 758, 000 cells is used in this study, with refined resolution
near the wall to maintain 𝑦 + < 1 along the airfoil surface in all the cases, see Figure 1. This mesh resolution was
consolidated after conducting a mesh convergence study at 2° angle of attack with Menter’s SST model, see Figure 2.
The angle of attack was varied between 2◦ to 14◦ at an increment of 2 and an additional 17◦ was considered in the stall
zone. The angle of attack was varied by changing the direction of freestream velocity vector in the inflow condition,
while keeping the geometry and mesh fixed.

A. The Spalart-Allamaras Model

simulate wall-bounded turbulent flows, particularly in aerospace applications. This model is an evolved model of
Nee-Kovansky model [22] and is inspired by Baldwin-Barth model [23]. It was designed for high Reynolds-number
flows, providing a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. The model has been widely used in RANS
simulations of external aerodynamic flows, including flow over airfoils, see e.g. [5]. However, it has limitations, including
its reduced accuracy in low Reynolds-number flows, inability to model flow transition and separation effectively [3].

IV. Uncertainty Quantification Approach
In general, there are two types of uncertainties: aleatory and epistemic [25]. The aleatory uncertainties are random,
inherent to a system, and usually cannot be fully removed. Examples are the fluctuations in the ambient temperature
and pressure when running a wind tunnel experiment. In contrast, the epistemic (systematic) uncertainties originate
from a lack of knowledge or modelling insufficiency and discrepancy. Therefore, they can be reduced by improving
our knowledge or modelling approach. In the context of the present study, the uncertainty due to the RANS models’
coefficients are epistemic. The general approach in UQ is to express such a problem in a stochastic framework to
apply statistical or probabilistic techniques. This means, we let the uncertain model coefficients to vary over prescribed
ranges, and then we quantify the resulting uncertainty propagated into the QoIs (here the lift and drag coefficients). As a
5

complement to this, global sensitivity analyses in the form of the Sobol indices [26] allow for quantifying the share of
each of the uncertain parameters in the resulting uncertainty in the QoIs. This section provides a brief description of
the methods employed for these purposes. It is noteworthy that all UQ analyses in this study are performed using the
open-source Python package UQit [27].
A. Polynomial Chaos Expansion Method
In this study, non-intrusive polynomial chaos expansion (NIPCE) method, see Ghanem and Spanos [28] and Eldred [29],
is employed for efficient uncertainty propagation from a limited number of realizations. The non-intrusive nature of
the method means that, first, a set of 𝑛 samples for the uncertain parameters q are drawn from their admissible space
Q ⊂ R 𝑝 . Then, the flow simulations are performed at each of such samples to obtain the associated values of the
QoIs. As thoroughly explained in Rezaeiravesh et al. [30], using the collected data, the NIPCE method constructs a
projection-based surrogate for the QoIs in the space of uncertain parameters q. The resulting surrogate reads as,

V. Results and Discussion
A. Uncertainty Quantification
First, the propagated uncertainty from the SA and SST models’ parameters into the computed lift and drag coefficients,
𝐶 𝐿 and 𝐶𝐷 , respectively, is discussed. Figure 3 shows the variation of the 𝐶 𝐿 with the angle of attack (AoA) for both
SA and SST RANS models. In the plots, the shaded areas show the 95% Confidence Interval (CI) around the stochastic
mean of the QoIs, obtained from the NIPCE. In addition, the experimental data [21] are added for validation of the
RANS predictions. For both the turbulence models, the uncertainty in 𝐶 𝐿 is insignificant up to AoA=10° and then
gradually increases up to 14°. Above this, a sharp increase in the 95% CI is observed due to the stall. This means that,
in the stall condition, both models become too sensitive to the choice of the coefficients. Nevertheless, no combination
of the modelling coefficients could result in accurate prediction of the 𝐶 𝐿 at large AoAs where separation occurs. This
is clear from the deviation between the mean prediction and the reference experimental data.
A similar trend is observed in the uncertainty of 𝐶𝐷 values, see Figure 4. The uncertainty in 𝐶𝐷 is low up to AoA of
12° and it significantly grows in the stall region. What is important is that, in general, the confidence interval for the SA
model is smaller than the SST model indicating more robustness of the SA model to the variation of its coefficients. In
contrast, for the SST model, the reference experimental data fall within the CI at all AoAs, while for the SA model, this
only happens for AoAs from 8° to 12°. This behaviour should be interpreted cautiously noting the potential uncertainty
in the experimental data is not included. Another important observation is that the sensitivity of the 𝐶𝐷 to the variation
of the modelling coefficients is more than that of the 𝐶 𝐿 for both RANS models and across all AoAs. This can be better
understood from Figure 5, where the normalized uncertainty (1.96*standard deviation/mean) in both QoIs is plotted at
different AoAs. Clearly, at any AoA the uncertainty in the 𝐶𝐷 is much higher than that in the 𝐶 𝐿 . For both QoIs, there
is a sharp increase in the uncertainty after the stall. What is also more evident is the higher uncertainty in the QoIs when
using the SST model. When interpreting this, we should also note that the uncertain parameters and their variation
range were not the same in the SA and SST models.
B. Sensitivity Analysis
The Sobol indices can quantify the contribution of the uncertain parameters to the propagated uncertainty in the QoIs.
Figure 6 shows the main and dual-interaction Sobol indices for the 𝐶 𝐿 and 𝐶𝐷 when the SA model is adopted. Looking
at the main indices, it is clear that for all AoAs except the highest one considered in this study, the von Kármán coefficient
𝜅 is the most influential parameter on both 𝐶 𝐿 and 𝐶𝐷 . This is only in the stall region, AoA greater than 14° that the
sensitivity with respect to 𝜅 decreases while seeing an increase with respect to 𝜎. These observations are consistent
with what is reported by Schaefer et al., [5, 6]. Interestingly, the sensitivity with respect to 𝐶𝑣1 remains almost constant
at all AoAs, although this parameter ranks second and third, in terms of influence, on the 𝐶 𝐿 and 𝐶𝐷 , respectively. A
main interpretation from these observations is that if there is only one parameter that can be altered to control the lift
and drag coefficients, it will be 𝜅. In contrast to the main indices, the dual interactions between the three parameters 𝜅,

VI. Conclusion and Future Work
A. Conclusion
This study conducted uncertainty quantification and global sensitivity analysis for two RANS turbulence models. The
uncertainties were introduced through three modelling parameters in each model. The quantities of interest were the lift
and drag coefficients of the NACA0012 airfoil in incompressible flow, computed from CFD simulations over a range of
angles of attack. The uncertainty propagation results showed that the uncertainty increases with angle of attack, with a
sharp rise near the stall. In general, Menter’s SST model [8] exhibited higher uncertainty (lower robustness) compared
to the Spalart–Allmaras (SA) model [7]. Overall, the drag coefficient 𝐶𝐷 showed a higher sensitivity to variations in the
RANS model parameters than the lift coefficient 𝐶 𝐿 .
The global sensitivity analysis, based on Sobol indices, showed that up to the stall the von Kármán coefficient 𝜅
is the most influential parameter driving the uncertainty in both 𝐶 𝐿 and 𝐶𝐷 when using the SA model. After stall,
the influence of 𝜅 and another parameter becomes nearly identical. For the SA model, interactions between model
coefficients were found to be insignificant at all angles of attack except beyond stall. For the SST model, no single
parameter consistently dominated the uncertainty in 𝐶 𝐿 across the angles of attack. In contrast, the parameter 𝛽∗ was
the most influential factor for 𝐶𝐷 before stall, while after stall the interaction between 𝛽∗ and 𝑎 1 became the dominant
contributor to uncertainty in 𝐶𝐷 . Overall, this study provides a systematic uncertainty quantification framework for
comparing RANS models when the influence of their parameters is of interest. Moreover, for the NACA0012 case
considered here, the analyses suggest that the SA model is more robust and easier to control than the SST model.
B. Future Work
This study was limited to two RANS models for the NACA0012 airfoil in incompressible flow regime and a fixed
Reynolds number. Future studies should focus on uncertainty quantification for multiple airfoils at different angles of
attack to see if similar trends emerge or if a slight change in geometry, as seen in different airfoils, change the sensitivity
trends. In addition to this, there is a need to analyze how the trends for epistemic uncertainty quantification and
