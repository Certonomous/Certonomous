Abstract
Experiments have been undertaken to investigate the natural convection of air in a tall dierentially heated rectangular cavity
(2.18 m high by 0.076 m wide by 0.52 m in depth). They were performed with temperature dierentials between the vertical plates of
19.6°C and 39.9°C, giving Rayleigh numbers based on the width of 0:86  106 and 1:43  106 . Under these conditions the ¯ow in the
core of the cavity is fully turbulent and property variations with temperature are comparatively small. A previously used experimental rig has been modi®ed, by ®tting partially conducting top and bottom walls and outer guard channels, to provide boundary
conditions which avoid the inadequately de®ned sharp changes in temperature gradient and other problems associated with insucient insulation on nominally adiabatic walls. Mean and turbulent temperature and velocity variations within the cavity have
been measured, together with heat ¯uxes and turbulent shear stresses. The temperature and ¯ow ®elds were found to be closely twodimensional, except close to the front and back walls, and anti-symmetric across the diagonal of the cavity. The partially conducting
roof and ¯oor provide locally unstable thermal strati®cation in the wall jet ¯ows there, which enhances the turbulence as the ¯ow
moves towards the temperature controlled plates. The results provide a greatly improved benchmark for the testing of turbulence
models in this low turbulence Reynolds number ¯ow. Ó 2000 Elsevier Science Inc. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
Natural convection within enclosures occurs in many
practical situations ranging from simple space heating of domestic rooms to parts of industrial and nuclear installations. In
typical applications, although much of the ¯ow is turbulent,
there are extensive regions with a low turbulent Reynolds
number, and often signi®cant regions of laminar ¯ow or regions of stagnation caused by local strati®cation. Moreover,
when the heat which drives the ¯ow is introduced or extracted
over approximately vertical boundaries, the thermal conditions there are close to constant heat ¯ux with distance from
the wall, but the shear stress varies rapidly and changes sign
close to the wall. For these reasons, simple turbulence modelling of such ¯ows, and particularly the use of standard wall
laws, is totally inappropriate.
Despite the widespread occurrence of natural convection in
enclosed regions, the availability of good quality experimental
data involving both temperature and velocity ®elds is still very
limited, e.g., Cheesewright et al. (1986), Dafa'Alla and Betts

Most experimental work has attempted to provide data
under idealized thermal boundary conditions, such as constant temperature or constant heat ¯ux active walls and
adiabatic or perfectly conducting passive ones. For example,
Dafa'Alla and Betts (1996) attempted to impose a constant
heat ¯ux on the hot wall, with an isothermal cold wall and all
other walls insulated. As discussed by Allard (1993) and
Fusegi and Hyun (1994), all solid insulators have thermal
conductivities greater than air, so that it was inevitably found
impossible to reproduce the ideals speci®ed. Dafa'Alla and
Betts encountered particular diculties at the junction between the electrically heated hot plate and the nominally
adiabatic top and bottom walls. These problems were typical
of all attempts to reproduce idealized boundary conditions,
and consequently, when the results were used by Ince et al.
(1993) to test low-Reynolds-number turbulence models,
measured wall temperatures had to be used as input rather
than the ideals speci®ed.
Although the results of Ince et al. (1993) were encouraging
to the turbulence modellers in general terms, the uncertainty in
boundary layer de®nition in regions of rapidly changing temperature gradient (e.g., at one end of the nominally adiabatic
top and bottom surfaces) meant that one does not know
whether the relatively minor dierences between experiment
and computation should be attributed to the turbulence model
or the problems with the experiment. The present experiments
were therefore designed to overcome these diculties and
provide an appropriate benchmark for modellers. The apparatus of Dafa'Alla and Betts (1996) has been rebuilt to ensure
well-de®ned, readily measurable, and smoothly varying temperatures within the boundary walls. Conduction within the
walls becomes part of the problem, but there is now a clear
unambiguous speci®cation. Moreover, the active walls are now
both eectively isothermal, the internal surface temperatures
of the top and bottom vary smoothly, heat loss through the
front and back walls is small, and the depth of the cavity is
sucient to produce eectively two-dimensional ¯ow over
most of the enclosure volume. The results have been used as a
benchmark for testing the numerical predictions of turbulence
models at the 5th and 7th ERCOFTAC workshops on re®ned
¯ow modelling (1996, 1998) and are presented here with more
detail for a wider audience. A summary of the results at mid-

2. Experimental apparatus and instrumentation
2.1. Apparatus
The apparatus is a modi®cation to that of Dafa'Alla and
Betts (1996), and is described in detail in Bokhari (1996). The
internal dimensions of the cavity shown diagrammatically on
Fig. 1(a) are 2.18 m high (H), 0.076 m wide between the hot
and cold walls (W) and 0.52 m deep (D). The hot and cold
plates are of polished aluminium backed by temperature
controlled water jackets and the water passes upwards along
channels behind each plate, with independent ¯ow control of
each channel to ensure equality of temperature across the
plates. Temperatures at entry were controlled to +/)0.05°C by
PID (REX, C10) controllers. For the cold plate it was found to
be most convenient to cool the cold tank continuously and
control the temperature by means of a supplementary (1 kW)
heater inserted in the well-mixed tank. The whole rectangular
cavity assembly is supported from a shaft at the back of the
hot plate, which is connected to the separate main support
frame. The cavity can therefore be tilted if required in the
future.
The active plates are oversized relative to the internal dimensions of the cavity as part of the isothermal control, as
shown on Fig. 1(b) and (c). The new hot plate is 2.38 m by 0.53
m and is backed by a 10 mm thick water jacket with four
channels. The water jacket is then insulated on its back with
1 cm thick closed-cell thermo-¯ux insulating material and
bolted to a cellular wooden frame with a 5 cm thickness of
polystyrene behind it, to further reduce heat losses. The cold
plate is a refurbishment of that used previously and is 2.40 m
by 0.53 m backed by a 4.8-mm water jacket with three channels. It is supported externally from the hot wall mainly at the
top and bottom, but with two bolts on the front and back of
the cavity to ensure a uniform width to the cavity. The cold
water jacket is backed by 5 cm of polystyrene.
The passive front and back walls are of 9.5 mm thick acrylic
sheet. The intention is that temperature gradients normal to
these faces should be small (close to adiabatic). However with
the existing instrumentation and supports, it was not possible
to provide full internal guard cavities of the sort used by
Mergui et al. (1993) and Tian (1997) without reducing the
depth dimension of the main cavity unacceptably. As an alternative, the acrylic walls were clamped externally by square
sectioned stainless steel hollow channels, shown on Fig. 1.
Water was circulated through these channels from the same
tanks as were used for the hot and cold plates and the whole
assembly backed by 5 cm polystyrene slabs. In this way an
isothermal boundary condition can be imposed in the plane of
the active plates right out to and through the passive walls.
Although heat loss through the acrylic walls is only minor, the
external boundary conditions are fully de®ned by means of a
strip of seven thermocouples which can be attached to the
acrylic sheet with good thermal contact at any elevation.

The top and bottom walls are constructed from 5 cm thick
natural hard rubber, with a layer of 35 thermocouples imbedded at mid-thickness. They are wedged by the active walls,
with good thermal contact ensured by a layer of heat conducting grease. The outer sides of the rubber walls are covered
with 10 cm of polystyrene insulation and the inner sides by a
thin (10 lm) sheet of adhesive aluminium foil, to reduce radiation absorption. The natural rubber has a thermal conductivity of 0.155 W/mK, which is close to the eective mean
conductivity across the cavity under the turbulent conditions

(i.e., Nusselt number times molecular conductivity of air).
Consequently, this avoids both the excessive drainage of heat
between the active walls, which would occur with ``perfectly
conducting'' walls, and the inadequately de®ned sharp temperature variations encountered close to an active wall in attempts at adiabatic conditions. As well as providing well
de®ned boundary conditions, the use of the rubber also provides unstable thermally strati®ed layers adjacent to both the
roof and the ¯oor, which oppose the re-laminarization which
might otherwise occur there (cf. Cheesewright et al., 1986).

2.2. Instrumentation
Temperatures in the water circulating in the water jackets
and guard channels were monitored by nine thermocouples on
the cold side and 21 on the hot. These, like those in the rubber
roof and ¯oor and on the outside of the acrylic passive walls,
are K-type and connected to switch boxes. A single traversed
K-type thermocouple was also used for measurements within
the turbulent air¯ow inside the cavity. The diameter of the
Chromel-Alumel wires is 75 lm diameter, which is robust
enough for long use and has a suciently rapid response time,
calculated to be 0.07 s (equivalent to 13 Hz). During this work,
the response time of the traversing thermocouple was also
measured directly by measuring the time history as the bead
was intermittently heated by a laser beam. This gave a response time of 0.065 s. Access for the internal thermocouple

traverses is through sealed slots in a vertical acrylic wall and
positional accuracy as distance from a wall is estimated to be
smaller than the wire thickness.
At each traverse position, 2048 samples were recorded in
128 s. The signal was ampli®ed by a factor of 1000 by a DANA
type 2850 DC ampli®er before passing to a DISA type 55D26
signal conditioner for ®nal ampli®cation and low pass ®ltering.
The signal was then digitized by a 12 bit OEI ADC unit to
provide a digital accuracy of about 0.1°C. This choice of
processing conditions re¯ects the known presence of signi®cant
low frequency components in the turbulent ¯ow. During the
course of the present work, spectral analyses of both the
temperature and velocity records were made. These indicated
that the high frequency cut o from the response time and the
methods of processing the signals had a negligible eect on the
recorded rms values.

The ®rst thing one notices about the results on Fig. 2 is the
high quality of the anti-symmetry, both across the cavity in
the mid-height region y=H  0:5 and between the top and the
bottom (y=H  0:05 and 0.95). A major cause of this was the
choice of the rubber top and bottom walls which produced
similar thermal strati®cations in the wall ¯ows there, which is
con®rmed by the traverses shown on Fig. 3. These traverses
indicate unstably strati®ed boundary layers with near-wall

travels horizontally. This raised level of ¯uctuation continues
passed the corners into the start region of the vertical ¯ows
along the active plates, as can be seen on Fig. 2 at y=H  0:95
on the cold side and y=H  0:05 on the hot side. It can also be
seen that, although the variation of temperature at the surface
of the top and bottom walls is not exactly linear between the
active walls, there are no sudden changes of gradient, which
can make interpolation of data so dicult (cf., Dafa'Alla and
Betts, 1996). As a consequence, the benchmark boundary
conditions can be chosen either as linear on the outer surfaces
of the rubber (or at the position of the thermo-couples imbedded within it) or by interpolation from the experimental
data at the surfaces.
An early examination of the temperature variations normal
to the active walls showed that in the ®rst 3 mm from the wall
(seven readings) the variation of temperature is closely linear
with distance from the wall. Thus, since heat ¯ux is constant
there, this region is within the molecular conduction layer and
wall temperatures can be accurately obtained by extrapolation,
while heat transfer can be determined from the wall gradient.
The validity of this way of measuring the surface heat ¯ux had
previously been con®rmed in the earlier experiments of Ince
(1985) and Dafa'Alla and Betts (1996), where the hot wall was
heated electrically. From a comparison of these surface temperatures with those obtained from the thermocouples in the
water jackets, it became apparent that there was a noticeable
thermal resistance on the water-side of the active plates, particularly on the older cold plate. Despite repeated ¯ushing with
the anti-corrosion additive Rodol NA1, it was not possible to
reduce the temperature dierence between the water and the
air-side of the cold plate to less than 0.9°C at the lower Rayleigh number, while dierences on the hot side were no more
than 0.3°C. The temperatures quoted in this paper are therefore the air-side temperatures obtained from extrapolation of
the air traverses. These have a maximum variation of +0.2/
)0.4°C about the mean on the cold side (compared with a
temperature rise in the ¯owing water of 0.3°C) and +/)0.2°C
(compared with 0.2°C) on the hot side. This is a comparatively
small deviation from isothermal conditions, as is shown on
Fig. 4(a). For maximum accuracy of the curve ®tting, use was
made of an analysis similar to that of Launder and Sharma
(1974). This shows that, in a constant heat ¯ux layer with the
mean velocity normal to the wall zero, the leading term from
the near wall turbulence in a polynomial for mean temperature
is the fourth power of distance from the wall. Account can also

be taken of a linear variation of thermal conductivity with
temperature by including a calculable second order term (no
cube term) in the expansion. One then obtains a fourth order
polynomial for mean temperature with no cubic term and only
three unknown coecients. Least squares ®tting for this
polynomial gave excellent agreement over the ®rst eight data
points, with typical standard errors of 0.07°C. Taking into
account also uncertainty in positional accuracy and thickness
