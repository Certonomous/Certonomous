The Fifth AIAA CFD High-Lift Prediction Workshop was held with the goal of assessing
the numerical prediction capability of current-generation computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
technology for swept, medium/high-aspect-ratio wings in high-lift configurations. A key aspect
of this endeavor was the use of Technology Focus Groups (TFG), which included both meshgeneration and flow-solver experts working together to accelerate advancements for their
particular CFD methodology, by addressing key questions of importance prior to the workshop.
The high-lift version of the NASA Common Research Model (CRM-HL) configuration was
the focus of this workshop, and was used for three unique test cases. Wind-tunnel data were
available for comparison for one of the test cases. Altogether, 365 datasets of CFD results
were submitted by 47 teams, with 41 teams contributing to the multiple configurations of
Case 1, 40 to Case 2, and 18 to Case 3. This paper provides a high-level summary of the
results and conclusions from the workshop. As concluded from past workshops, fixed-grid
Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes methods continued to be inaccurate and inconsistent for
high-lift flows near maximum lift. However, application of mesh-adaptation technology helped to
achieve improved consistency. Scale-resolving methods appeared most promising for predicting
high-lift flow physics, particularly at maximum lift. Best practices for these methods were
refined over the course of the workshop and new challenges were identified.

II. Introduction
he High-Lift Prediction Workshop (HLPW) series has been ongoing since 2010. Its goals include: (1) assess
the numerical prediction capability (meshing, numerics, turbulence modeling, high-performance computing
requirements, etc.) of current-generation CFD technology/codes for swept, medium-to-high-aspect ratio wings for
landing/take-off (high-lift) configurations; (2) develop practical modeling guidelines for CFD prediction of high-lift
flow fields; (3) determine the elements of high-lift flow physics that are critical for modeling to enable the development
of more accurate prediction methods and tools; and (4) enhance CFD prediction capability for practical high-lift
aerodynamic design and optimization. The fifth workshop, HLPW-5, was held in Las Vegas in summer 2024, associated
with the AIAA Aviation Forum.
Previous workshop summaries can be found in their respective summary papers [1–9]. Many of these papers include
references to other individual participant CFD studies as well. A high-level overview of the workshop series is also
available [10]. Generally speaking, over the nearly 15 years since the first workshop in 2010, there has been a significant
increase in available computational power, allowing most participants to run on significantly larger CFD grids than were
previously possible. Because of this,improved grid-generation codes, and more robust CFD algorithms/capabilities, it
has become easier to include all the geometry components (such as slat and flap brackets) of very complex high-lift
configurations. Despite these improvements, CFD solutions based on the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS)
equations have failed as a whole to accurately and consistently predict high-lift characteristics near 𝐶 𝐿,𝑚𝑎𝑥 , as compared
to experimental observations. Mostly, this is attributed to the inability of RANS turbulence models to accurately model
the flow physics associated with highly separated flows.
Starting around the time of HLPW-3 in 2017, it was recognized that scale-resolving simulation (SRS) methods
had the potential to predict high-lift flowfields more accurately than RANS technology. In HLPW-4 two of the five
Technology Focus Groups (TFGs) were organized to investigate both Hybrid RANS/Large-Eddy Simulation (HRLES)
methods and Wall-Modeled LES (WMLES) methods, respectively. In that workshop, the results obtained in the HRLES
and WMLES TFGs were reasonably successful at reproducing flowfields near 𝐶 𝐿,𝑚𝑎𝑥 , generally agreeing better with
experimental data than RANS results. Efforts in these two TFGs have continued into the current workshop to further
explore, develop, and document SRS capabilities and best practices to predict high-lift flowfields.
The other three TFGs from HLPW-4, fixed-grid RANS, mesh adaptation (ADAPT), and high-order discretization
(HO), also actively participated and contributed to HLPW-5. However, the HO TFG did not directly participate in flow
solver studies, but instead focused primarily on the development of high-order meshes.
HLPW-5 was made up of three separate test cases of varying complexity. Each test case was evaluated sequentially
by all TFGs together, with approximately six months dedicated to each individual case, and a mini-workshop conducted
at the conclusion of test cases 1 and 2 to collectively review results. The test cases were designed such that lessons
learned were nominally directly applicable to the future cases, and the mini-workshops enabled collaborative learning as
the workshop progressed. This approach allowed participants to focus, together, on specific issues pertinent to one test
case at a time, and greatly enhanced collaboration compared to previous workshops, where participants either worked in
isolation or worked across a larger volume of test cases simultaneously.
The current paper documents the major high-level lessons learned from HLPW-5 across all of the TFGs. Companion
papers from each TFG provide more technical details and results [11–15]. This paper is organized as follows. Section III
provides a description of the test cases, Section IV summarizes the CFD meshes, and Section V summarizes the entries.
Then, Section VI describes results, Section VII highlights the key takeaways, and the paper concludes with Section VIII.

III. Description of Test Cases
There were three unique test cases that were considered for HLPW-5, which each utilized different configurations
of the High-Lift Common Research Model (CRM-HL) [16–18]. The CRM-HL is an open-source, publicly available
commercial transport aircraft geometry representative of an industrial high-lift configuration, utilized for CFD validation
within a broad international CRM-HL Ecosystem [19]. Specific test case geometry for this workshop either reflects the
CRM-HL reference configuration, or explicitly matches the “as-designed” geometric CAD definition of one particular
wind-tunnel model that was designed, built, and tested by an Ecosystem partner. The CRM-HL geometry was provided
to HLPW-5 participants in full-scale inches, for which the MAC is 275.8 inches. In this paper, any mention of X, Y, or
Z coordinates is in this same reference frame.

The three test cases chosen for HLPW-5 were developed and organized to increase complexity sequentially, and
were intended to help answer specific key questions aimed at advancing the goals of the workshop. Each test case and
its specific objectives are described in the sections below.
A. Test Case 1: CRM-HL Wing-Body Verification
Previous High-Lift Prediction Workshops have highlighted the importance of solver verification to obtain consistent
solutions for complex flow problems. For this workshop, the verification problem was chosen based on the simplest
CRM-HL wing-body configuration (CRM-HL-WB), and was done in coordination with the AIAA High-Fidelity CFD
Verification Workshop [20]. While the full CRM-HL configuration features surfaces typical of a commercial aircraft in
landing configuration, including slats, flaps, nacelle, horizontal tail, vertical tail, and landing gear, the CRM-HL-WB
configuration chosen for Test Case 1 removes most of this complexity, leaving just the wing and body. This configuration
is shown in Fig. 1, and the test case parameters are given in Table 1.

Fig. 1

CRM-HL-WB Configuration for Test Case 1

The high level objective for test case 1 is to demonstrate grid convergence on the simplest configuration, before
introducing the geometric complexities associated with high-lift devices. Beyond grid convergence, exploration of
sensitivities to solver settings specific to particular methodologies (for example, turbulence model choice) is encouraged.
It is the hope that lessons learned on this test case are directly applicable to the more complicated test cases that follow.
Key questions for test case 1 are:
1) Are RANS solvers able to demonstrate verification on this problem and series of grids?
2) For Non-RANS solvers, what is the most consistent approach to grid families that can demonstrate a trend
towards grid independence on this problem?
3) Is there enough consistency among non-RANS approaches that there is reasonable agreement on a gridindependent solution?
4) Does the ensemble of results among modeling approaches compared to the experimental free-air corrected data
tell us anything useful about uncertainty?
In order to meet the objectives of the High-Fidelity CFD Verification Workshop, the RANS participants were highly
encouraged to run the SA-neg-R(crot1)-QCR2000 turbulence model[21], and utilize a consistent set of grids provided,
3

which are further described in Section IV.
For Non-RANS participants, particularly those utilizing scale-resolving techniques in the WMLES and HRLES
TFGs, the standard approaches to verification become challenging due to the use of different grid topologies, temporal
discretization techniques, duration of simulations and averaging windows, and even equation sets. As a result, there is
typically not enough overlap in related results to meaningfully declare any one specific solution approach the standard.
Non-RANS participants were encouraged to demonstrate grid convergence using multiple levels of grid, along with best
practice solver settings, while monitoring integrated force and moment convergence. Gridding requirements for these
TFGs were purposefully left vague, such that participants could uniquely define how to best design grid families to help
answer the key questions.
Results from Test Case 1 were intended to be compared to full-model test data obtained using the Boeing 6%
CRM-HL at the QinetiQ wind-tunnel facility. Due to unforeseen manufacturing delays, the test data was unavailable in
time for the workshop. There was also no CRM-HL-WB test data available from other Ecosystem partners, including
the ONERA F1 test heavily referenced for Test Case 2. Consequently, Test Case 1 was done entirely computationally,
without a comparison to experimental data at this time. Future workshops may elect to revisit this problem after
experimental data is collected.
B. Test Case 2: CRM-HL Configuration Buildup
Test Case 2 attempts to assess the ability of CFD to predict the effect of varying geometric fidelity through
component build-up to help isolate specific types of flow physics associated with high-lift aerodynamics. The CRM-HL
is designed such that components can be added to the wing-body configuration one at a time, to expose individual flow
features systematically, in contrast to attempting to parse out all of the complex flow interactions from the full landing
configuration. This test case additionally expands beyond test case 1 by requiring full lift polars consisting of 5 to 7
angles of attack, instead of just a single point.
Test case 2.1 begins with similar geometry to test case 1, the CRM-HL-WB configuration, but now includes the
horizontal tail, vertical tail, and flap support fairings. Test case 2.2 adds a full span leading-edge slat to test case 2.1.
This greatly increases the angle of attack at which the wing stalls, and exposes the wing to additional complex flow
features such as a slat wake and pressure losses due to slat brackets. Test case 2.3 adds both an inboard and outboard
trailing-edge flap with the same positioning as the landing reference geometry, serving to greatly increase the total
circulation of the configuration. Finally, test case 2.4 adds the pylon and nacelle (with a chine), and is equivalent to the
CRM-HL-LDG reference configuration. This series of configurations are shown in Fig. 2.
While Test Case 1 was intended to help develop modeling practices including grid refinement techniques and
numerical modeling choices, Test Case 2 was chosen to help refine those practices. By directly comparing numerical
solutions to experimental data in this systematic manner, one can begin to link simulation accuracy to the presence of a
single geometric feature, and update modeling techniques developed to improve simulation accuracy. Key questions for
Test Case 2 revolve around this approach:
1) Does the consistency in integrated forces and moments from CFD simulations improve when modeling
geometrically simpler high-lift configurations?
2) If accuracy falls off due to the presence of a single component, can better modeling approaches be established to
improve the predictions?
This test case is designed to utilize CRM-HL configuration geometry that matches experimental data collected
for the equivalent as-designed geometry tested in the ONERA F1 wind-tunnel [22]. As mentioned previously, this
experiment did not collect data on the wing-body configuration. Rather, it was intended that CFD results for Test Case
2.1 would be compared to data obtained from a test on the Boeing 6% model in the QinetiQ 5-metre tunnel. CFD
simulations were requested to match the free-air data available from ONERA’s wind-tunnel tests, with test parameters
documented in Table 2. Further, Test Case 2 was conducted semi-blind, where experimental data for selected anchor
points for configurations 2.2 through 2.4 were provided in advance. Once most initial results were computed and
submitted, full datasets from the experiment were released to participants.

IV. Overview of CFD Meshes
Over 245 individual meshes, comprising about 37 mesh families, were created and shared for this workshop. Most
of these were made by mesh generation experts who were connected with one or more of the TFGs. Some meshes were
also shared by participants. Mesh-generation guidelines from previous workshops were available forcreating RANS
meshes, but the mesh-generation experts were not constrained to follow these. In many cases, lessons learned during the
TFG activities and virtual meetings led to new meshing approaches. All meshes shared by participants are available on
the HLPW website [23], though several were used by participants that weren’t shared, particularly those that didn’t
follow the traditional unstructured paradigms.
Figure 3 shows the mesh size ranges for the various families of grids made available for the three test cases in
HLPW-5. For example, among the RANS grids created for Case 1 (red squares in the left-most section of the figures),
the coarsest grids (open symbols and dashed lines) had around 1 million cells and 1 million nodes, while the finest grids
(filled symbols and solid lines) had around 2 billion cells and over 300 million nodes. The meshes for Cases 2 and 3
tended to be somewhat larger than those for Case 1, due to the increased complexity of the configurations. The HO
meshes were generally more coarse than the standard meshes created for the other TFGs; this is because HO meshes
contain additional degrees of freedom within each of its cells. It is noteworthy that there were many meshes created
with over a billion nodes or cells. The largest contributed mesh was a WMLES grid created for one of the Case 2
configurations, containing 6.2 billion cells and 2.3 billion nodes.
Most CFD practitioners are very familiar with RANS grids and gridding practices. RANS meshes are generally
body-fitted, and tend to have very fine normal spacing at walls, typically with the goal of having a 𝑦 + near unity at the
first grid point or cell center (this spacing relaxes as you move away from the wall). The streamwise and spanwise
spacings near walls are generally much larger, albeit with clustering in important regions like leading and trailing-edges.
The end result is a mesh with cells near walls that are like pancakes, very flat in shape, with very large aspect ratios.
Meshes for HRLES still require RANS-like fine spacing near walls, but in the regions where LES is expected to be
active, the cells optimally should be fine enough to resolve the turbulent eddies and are more isotropic in shape. In
contrast, WMLES modeling best practices suggest cells which tend to be fairly isotropic in shape (with aspect ratios
typically less than 5) everywhere within the boundary layer or within active turbulent shear layers/wakes. A typical
WMLES grid may have on the order of 20 cells across the boundary layer, although this can be very challenging to
achieve (nearly impossible with today’s resources) near the front of wings where the boundary layers are extremely thin.

V. Summary of Entries
Over the course of the high-lift prediction workshops, the number of active participants and submitted test cases
has steadily increased. In HLPW-1, 21 groups submitted 39 entries; in HLPW-2, 26 groups submitted 48 entries; in
HLPW3, 35 groups submitted 79 entries; and in HLPW-4, 44 groups submitted 184 entries.
In HLPW-5, its three test cases were computed sequentially by the TFGs over more than a year leading up to the
workshop. Some teams computed all three cases, while others did not. Two “mini-workshops” were held virtually to
discuss interim results for Cases 1 and 2. Altogether, 47 groups submitted 365 entries, with 41 groups contributing to
Case 1, 40 to Case 2, and 18 to Case 3. The entries are summarized in Table 4, where the particular TFG is denoted in
the entry number by a letter: R for RANS, A for ADAPT, H for HO, L for HRLES, and W for WMLES. It should be
noted that the WMLES TFG included two groups that did not use WMLES: W-001 used the unsteady Euler equations on
adapted meshes and W-009 used a Lattice-Boltzmann scheme with a very large-eddy simulation (VLES) methodology.

VI. Workshop Results
The workshop results are covered here in three sections, one for each test case. They are specifically addressed
in terms of Key Questions (KQs) that were posed prior to the workshop’s activities. The KQs were written in order
to focus on particular aspects of the computations, which, if answered satisfactorily, could significantly advance the
high-lift community’s knowledge.
A. Test Case 1 Results
1. Are RANS solvers able to demonstrate verification on this problem and series of grids?
Case 1 does not yet have experimental data associated with it, but from the point of view of verification, this is not
necessary, since we are only concerned with determining the “truth” solution for a given RANS turbulence model. The
RANS TFG for this case ended up focusing primarily on results using the standard Spalart-Allmaras (SA), SA-neg and
the SA-neg-R(crot1)-QCR2000 turbulence models (Note that the "neg" variant does not change the solution, so that the
standard SA and SA-neg solutions are considered together). Other turbulence models were used by a few participants,
but there were not enough data samples to be able to draw any conclusions about verification. However, for the two SA
variants, a number of different codes demonstrated deep iterative convergence on all levels for a particular grid family,
and subsequently showed grid convergence toward a common solution for each model.
Plots of all submitted RANS solutions (including different models and variants, solutions with poor iterative
convergence, and solutions on non-optimal grid families) are shown in Fig. 4. The spread in the asymptoting solutions,
as 𝑁 −1/3 → 0, was approximately Δ𝐶 𝐿 = 0.02, Δ𝐶𝐷 = 0.0040, and Δ𝐶 𝑀 = 0.02. (A few outliers were even outside of
this range.)

In contrast, codes that exhibited better mesh convergence trends tended to converge to a common solution. Results
for the SA-neg and SA-neg-R(crot1)-QCR2000 models are shown in Fig. 5. For SA-neg, 12 different results were seen to
asymptote toward a very narrow solution range for lift, drag, and moment as 𝑁 −1/3 → 0. The range was approximately
Δ𝐶 𝐿 = 0.005, Δ𝐶𝐷 = 0.0005, and Δ𝐶 𝑀 = 0.003. In the figures, different colored lines represent different mesh
families, while RANS results on adaptive meshes are shown with a gradient symbol. These results indicate that different
codes can display different grid convergence trajectories on the same mesh family. On the other hand, the trajectory can
also be influenced by the particular mesh when using a given code. However, the particular trajectory is not important for
verification; what matters is that the grid-converged answer is consistent for all codes as any given mesh is consistently
refined.
For the SA-neg-R(crot1)-QCR2000 turbulence model, seven different participant results agreed well with each other
as 𝑁 −1/3 → 0. The range was approximately Δ𝐶 𝐿 = 0.004, Δ𝐶𝐷 = 0.0006, and Δ𝐶 𝑀 = 0.002. Notably, this model’s
verified solutions were somewhat different from those of SA-neg, indicating that this verification process can distinguish
between different turbulence model variants.
The answer to this KQ is yes, correctly coded RANS solvers are able to demonstrate verification on this problem,

2. If accuracy falls off due to the presence of a single component, can better modeling approaches be established to
improve the predictions?
The answer to this KQ also depends on the CFD method used. For RANS methods, there was no convincing
evidence that RANS turbulence models can reliably predict separated flows. However in studying the “pizza slice”
wedge separation patterns, evidence suggests modeling approaches for this type of separation may be heavily influenced
by the level of residual convergence in the simulation. For HRLES, simpler test cases exposed known weaknesses
in mesh convergence using current DDES shielding function methods, particularly for flows where there are strong
adverse pressure gradients. The Deck-Renard function was utilized by several participants and was shown to improve
the robustness of the DDES method for Test Case 1 as the mesh was refined (see solid red line in Fig. 6(a)).
For WMLES, the systematic underprediction in CLmax from many participants led to increased attention to boundary
layer transition initiation and modeling, and particularly the importance in modeling the correct boundary layer on
the slat near 𝐶 𝐿,𝑚𝑎𝑥 . Focus on this area led to the implementation and testing of transition sensor models in several
codes. In general, the inclusion of a transition sensor in WMLES modeling dramatically impacts results near 𝐶 𝐿,𝑚𝑎𝑥 , as
shown in Fig. 17 from Test Case 2.4. However, there is still much work to be done to determine whether these sensor
approaches provide a general predictive capability.
3. Are there any conclusions that can be made regarding accuracy of one particular method over another?
The answer here is yes. One good example where there is a clear superior method over others is in the prediction of
post-stall aerodynamics. When accurately predicting the flow post stall, it is critical that the simulation properly models
not only the integrated forces and moments, but also the correct mechanism by which the flow breaks down due to
flow separation. In this case, the scale resolving methods (either HRLES or WMLES) are clearly superior. All RANS
simulations, including those employing mesh adaptation, failed to capture the correct flow features, while the HRLES
and WMLES results properly model the inboard flow separation and slat bracket wake behavior associated with the flow
breakdown mechanism observed in the experimental data (see Fig. 18).

VII. Key Findings
The fifth High-Lift Prediction Workshop set out to answer a number of key questions discussed in Section VI. In
taking a step back from the details, there are a number of key findings that are beyond the scope of the original key
questions. This section highlights these additional findings by test case.
A. Test Case 1 Key Findings
In looking at trends over the series of five workshops since 2010, progress towards consistency using RANS
methodologies is considerable. In the early High-Lift Prediction Workshops, participants had not yet converged on
gridding settings, solver settings, or convergence criterion, nor had the importance of verification of turbulence model
implementation been realized. As a result, spread in 𝐶 𝐿 , even in the linear region, could be upwards of 0.2, as shown in
Fig. 25. In the present workshop, test case 1 was purposely chosen to be challenging, at the edge or beyond the linear
range of the lift curve where the flow is on the verge of separation. Despite the added challenge, participants in the
RANS TFG were able to demonstrate consistency in lift across solvers to within 0.005 in 𝐶 𝐿 . This is a remarkable
improvement since the early workshops, and allows for robust conclusions to be made about modeling techniques and
best practices without being clouded by uncertainties due to gridding, model implementation, and convergence.
Beyond RANS, this test case proved more challenging than initially intended. While it was originally selected to
focus on grid convergence on a relatively simple problem, this test case exposed a number of modeling challenges for
scale-resolving solvers. The scale-resolving solutions consistently identified trailing-edge flow separation but revealed
significant sensitivity to modeling choices, which affected the extent of separation. As shown in Fig. 9, WMLES showed
grid convergence towards two distinct solution families, each with varying degrees of trailing-edge separation. These
variations, largely driven by differences in modeling choices that influence boundary layer development, are further
explored in [14]. Unlike RANS, which has been extensively used in previous workshops, scale-resolving techniques have
only been rigorously applied and evaluated since HLPW-4. As a result, there has not yet been sufficient time to develop
and demonstrate consistent modeling approaches for this class of problems using these methods. Interestingly, a test case
that consistently achieves a single solution across solvers in RANS can be much more challenging for scale-resolving
methods, due to sensitivities to factors not typically modeled in RANS, such as boundary layer transition.
B. Test Case 2 Findings
From the outset [16, 17], the CRM-HL was designed to be modular, by facilitating the addition and removal of
geometric components to systematically assess the impact of isolated flow features on vehicle aerodynamics. To date,
test campaigns at ONERA [22], and NASA [30] have collected experimental data on these configurations. Test Case 2
22

marks one of the first systematic approaches to study component build-up effects. Some of these results are highlighted
in Section VI, but it is worth highlighting the general struggles of all methods when it came to predicting the effects
of separated flow on the trailing-edge flap, as was shown in Fig. 14 and 15. This flow feature was previously studied
through the flap deflection test case in HLPW-4 [4], and is also specifically targeted in the current Test Case 2. These
cases conclusively demonstrate that there is much more work to be done to understand how best to capture the flow
environment present on deployed trailing-edge flaps at moderate angles of attack.
Documented in section VI and emphasized here, is the importance of boundary layer transition for scale resolving
methods. Participants in this workshop demonstrated that when using wall modeled approaches, the flow breakdown
over the wing at 𝐶 𝐿,𝑚𝑎𝑥 is very sensitive to the development of the boundary layer over the leading-edge elements.
Treating a portion of the slat boundary layer as laminar resulted in higher maximum lift, in better agreement with the
experiment, as previously shown in Figure 17. This sensitivity highlights the strong need for experimental data focused
on characterizing the laminar flow extent on the leading-edge slats. The evidence from WMLES participants, along with
results from the early workshops [1, 2], suggests that a deeper focus on transitional behavior is required in the future, as
it is likely that the sensitivity demonstrated in in the WMLES TFG is not strongly associated with wall modeling, rather
it is inherent in high-lift flow physics.
Lastly, it is worth mentioning the high computational cost of entry for this workshop. Across all test cases, workshop
organizers requested solutions for 51 different test points across 6 unique geometries, along with sensitivity studies
to both modeling parameters and gridding. This workshop demonstrated the value in computing the full lift polar as
opposed to a subset of individual points, but it is also acknowledged that the number of test points was very large.
Because of the availability of such a wealth of computational results, much more effort is required to adequately mine
the data and fully digest what can be learned from these CFD studies. To this end, future workshops will need to
reevaluate the quantity of test cases and/or data requested, and strongly consider revisiting test cases from previous
workshops to allow for deeper focus.
Scale resolving simulations, in particular, have continued to show great promise in predicting aerodynamic
performance across the range of test cases in this workshop, but simulations using these methods come at a high
computational cost, often orders of magnitude above RANS methods. Grid convergence studies completed on some of
the test cases in this workshop clearly demonstrate that there is little value in running under-resolved scale resolving
simulations, as the results can trend away from the experimental data in a non-linear manner. It must be acknowledged
that these approaches, while increasingly superior to RANS, are not yet affordable for all workshop participants.
C. Test Case 3 Key Findings
One of the objectives of Test Case 3 was to better understand CFD gridding requirements as a function of Reynolds
number. For most computational methods that solve to the wall, and for which the first grid cell spacing is typically set
to 𝑦 + of 1, the question about grid resolution is fairly well understood. However, for approaches using wall modeling it
is less so. There are many estimates in the literature for wall modeled gridding requirements [31], typically suggesting
that the number of cells required will increase significantly as the Reynolds number increases. Although there was no

23

experimental data to compare against, a few participants in the WMLES TFG were able to demonstrate comparable
trends in grid convergence between Reynolds numbers of 5.49 and 30 million. Simply put, grid convergence for
simulations run at the two Reynolds numbers on the same grids appeared to be roughly equivalent (see Figure 19(c) and
(d)). This finding is inconsistent with previous estimates, as well as physical intuition, and therefore warrants further
attention in future workshops. Analysis of test case 3 computed boundary layer profiles may also provide some insight.
One of the less positive takeaways from this particular test case was the lack of consistency demonstrated when there
was no experimental data with which to copare. This is evident in the spread in computational results between Test Case
3.2 in Figure 21 and Test Case 2.4 in Figure 16. Using two very comparable geometries, CFD results computed for
Test Case 3.2, where no experimental data was available, demonstrated larger differences between participants than in
Test Case 2.4, where experimental data was provided. From this observation, it may appear that there is no general
consensus on modeling or gridding practices that are suitable for blind prediction. Because of a smaller number of
samples available for this test case, there is clearly more work required in this area.
Lastly, despite the large spread in results, when comparing the predicted trends in Reynolds number from participant
to participant, there was reasonably good agreement with expectations in terms of lift at angle of attack. This was shown
in Figure 24, and discussed further in Section VI.

VIII. Summary
The 5th High-Lift Prediction Workshop continued the use of TFGs and collaborative meetings over the course of
more than a year prior to the workshop, to accelerate the maturation of computational methods to predict high-lift
aerodynamics. One key addition to the logistics of this workshop was performing the Test Cases one at a time, such
that all TFGs were focused on a single test case for a longer duration of time, then holding focused mini-workshops to
collaboratively discuss results for that test case across TFGs before moving onto the next test case. Collaboration in this
manner was found to be very successful, and enabled many issues and findings to be explored jointly.
Test Case 1 consisted of a single test point using the CRM-HL wing-body configuration, executed in conjunction
with the AIAA High-Fidelity CFD Verification Workshop, to explore grid convergence and verification for RANS
solvers using a single turbulence model. It also served as a challenging case to demonstrate grid convergence and
explore solver settings for methodologies beyond RANS. Importantly, many RANS participants were able to demonstrate
verification on this problem using a common turbulence model. Other TFGs were less consistent, hampered by issues
such as boundary layer transition, which prevented the HRLES and WMLES TFGs from achieving consensus on a
consistent solution.
The second test case for HLPW-5 focused on model build up, as envisioned for the original model architecture of
the CRM-HL. This test case consisted of four geometric configurations of increasing complexity, with the computation
of full lift polars required for each. Although the computational resource requirements were large, most participants
were able to complete the full request. Across TFGs, participants struggled in particular with predicting the flow
features associated with the addition of the trailing-edge flap, highlighting an area where future workshops should focus.
Results were sensitive to boundary layer transition on the leading-edge elements, suggesting the need for additional
experimental data to better characterize the boundary layer flow and enable further refinement of predictive capability.
As with previous workshops, RANS methodologies failed to consistently predict maximum lift, but are fairly consistent
in the linear range of the lift versus angle of attack relationship. Adaptive RANS was able to demonstrate consistent
grid convergence in the linear range using fewer points than required for hand-built grids. Scale resolving methods
including WMLES and HRLES were more consistent at predicting maximum lift, but that improved accuracy comes at
a drastically increased computational cost.
Test Case 3 explored Reynolds number trends from 1 million up to 30 million chord Reynolds number. This test case
was intended to be compared to data collected in the NASA NTF, but inconsistencies found between the experimental
dataset and other CRM-HL experimental results led to not having the NTF experimental data available for HLPW-5.
Despite this, several WMLES participants produced results suggesting that grid convergence may be achievable, even at
the highest Reynolds numbers. Both RANS and WMLES participants were able to produce lift versus Reynolds number
trends in line with expectations.
There was limited participation from the High-Order TFG. These methodologies continue to hold promise,
particularly in reducing computational cost for scale resolving methods. However, maturation of these technologies
continue to lag other approaches.
Finally, one takeaway from earlier workshops was the necessity to run in-tunnel simulations for proper validation.
However, this workshop largely deviated from that guidance, both because Test Case 2 was a full model and tunnel

of CFD simulations requested for this workshop were overwhelming for most participants. It is expected that future
workshops will revisit a subset of these test cases, and dive deeper into topics such as running in tunnel, and boundary
layer transition.
