\vspace{2pt}
{\footnotesize\textbf{The lumped resistance estimate is high by
$1.90\times$ to $3.54\times$.} Both closed-form variants overshoot, and they
overshoot by more than the $1.763\times$ spread between them --- so this is not
a matter of picking the wrong correlation. The overshoot is \emph{largest at
the lowest airspeed} ($3.54\times$ at $10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, falling to
$3.24\times$ at $40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$). \emph{Why:} the hand method collapses
the whole cooling path onto one resistance taken from a fully-developed
correlation, while the real passage is a short annular gap in which the flow is
still developing and the surface removes heat considerably better than the
correlation allows.}

{\footnotesize
$\bullet$ \textbf{All sixteen points met success criteria fixed before running.}
The acceptance limits, the tolerance on each predicted temperature and the
labels were written down before the first solver started; sixteen of sixteen
points met them.\\
$\bullet$ \textbf{The trends are monotonic and consistent across the whole
map.} Temperature rises in proportion to heat load at fixed airspeed and falls
steeply with airspeed at fixed load, with no reversal anywhere.\\
$\bullet$ \textbf{No grid-refinement check is available and none is claimed} ---
every point ran on one mesh. \textbf{No energy-conservation check is available
for this configuration and none is claimed.}}

{\footnotesize\textbf{Read this before you use the numbers}\\[1pt]
$\bullet$ One mesh level per point, so there is no numerical error bar on any
temperature in Table 1.\\
$\bullet$ Thermal radiation is not modelled, and no bound on how much it would
change these temperatures has been computed.\\
$\bullet$ Buoyancy is switched off. Whether forced airflow genuinely dominates
is not established here, and the low-airspeed column is where that assumption
is least comfortable.\\
$\bullet$ Material properties are representative values, not measured for your
parts.\\
$\bullet$ The duct is treated as an axisymmetric sector; no circumferential
features are represented.\\
$\bullet$ Near-wall mesh resolution meets its target at $10$ and
$20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and exceeds it at $30$ and $40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$,
limiting what may be said about surface heat-transfer coefficients; the
temperature bound above is unaffected.\\
$\bullet$ Nothing is claimed between the four airspeeds solved.}

{\footnotesize
\rolesig{Lead Researcher.} The result worth your attention is not the map, it is
the correction to the sizing method. A lumped resistance estimate of this duct
runs high by a factor of two to three and a half at every airspeed we solved ---
a systematic bias, not scatter, and worst at the low-speed end where a designer
is most likely to be nervous. We would not size this machine from a hand
resistance model again.

\rolesig{Lead Engineer.} At $305\,\mathrm{W}$ and $10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ ---
the hardest corner we solved --- the housing sits $96.4\,\mathrm{K}$ below your
$200\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ limit, and the whole $80$--$305\,\mathrm{W}$ range
clears both that limit and your $120\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ design target at all
four airspeeds. Between $80$ and $305\,\mathrm{W}$ at any airspeed in Table 1
you may scale the temperature by proportion. Do not read between the airspeeds,
and do not treat Table 3's extrapolated loads as a rating.

\rolesig{Lead Numericist.} The uncertainty column of Table 1 is empty on
purpose. Each point was solved once, on one mesh; an error bar needs the same
point on a refined mesh, we did not run one, so there is no honest number to put
there and we have not invented one. What the sixteen points do support is that
they met criteria written down in advance and that the trends are monotonic
throughout. A quantified numerical uncertainty here is a mesh-refinement study
--- say so and we will price it.}
