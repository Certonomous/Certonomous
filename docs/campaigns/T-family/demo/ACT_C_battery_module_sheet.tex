\vspace{1.5pt}
\textbf{1. Before we spend anything on your module: what our transient
conduction machinery is worth}

\vspace{1pt}
{\footnotesize We solved a three-dimensional transient conduction problem that
has a closed-form answer --- a uniform block, initially hot, cooled on its faces
by a fixed convective coefficient: the same physics as a cell losing heat to a
cooling channel --- and compared three independent readings of our solution
against that exact answer. Temperatures are the dimensionless excess
$\theta = (T-T_{\infty})/(T_{0}-T_{\infty})$ at Biot number $Bi = 1.0$ and
Fourier number $Fo = 0.2$. Success criteria, the tolerance on each reading and
the labels were fixed before the first solver started.}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Table 1 --- Solved against the exact conduction solution.} Three
quantities, three mesh levels each ($20$, $40$, $80$ cells per side; finest
$512\,000$ cells).

\vspace{2pt}
{\fontsize{7.2}{8.5}\selectfont
\begin{tabular}{l rr r r r r r}
\toprule
Reading of the solution & Exact & Solved & Deviation & Tolerance & Grid-refinement & Observed & Refinement \\
(dimensionless $\theta$, --) & value & (finest) & from exact, \% & used, \% & uncertainty, \% & order, -- & ratio, -- \\
\midrule
Average through the block        & 0.6175896496 & 0.6176037117 & $+0.00228$ & 45.5 & 0.0021 & 1.9996 & 3.999 \\
Temperature at the block centre  & 0.8591137894 & 0.8591831159 & $+0.00807$ & 32.3 & 0.0104 & 2.0006 & 4.002 \\
Temperature at a cooled face     & 0.5814449853 & 0.5815192620 & $+0.01277$ & 42.6 & 0.0154 & 2.0130 & 4.036 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}

\vspace{2pt}
{\footnotesize\textbf{All three readings are verified against the exact
conduction solution to within $0.013\,\%$} --- the largest of the three
deviations from the exact value, not an average and not a tolerance. Each also
used less than half of the tolerance allowed for it before the run (worst
$45.5\,\%$). The grid-refinement column is the numerical error bar on the
finest-mesh value, largest $0.0154\,\%$. Observed order is how fast the error
falls under mesh refinement: $1.9996$/$2.0006$/$2.0130$ against a theoretical
$2.000$, with errors falling by $3.999$/$4.002$/$4.036$ against a theoretical
$4.000$ for halving the cell size. \textbf{Second-order accurate by measurement,
not by assertion.}}

% ------------------------------------------------------------ caveats -------
\vspace{1.5pt}
{\color{rulegrey}\rule{\textwidth}{0.5pt}}\\[1pt]
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.60\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\colorbox{boxgrey}{\begin{minipage}{0.97\textwidth}
{\footnotesize\textbf{Read this before you use anything on this sheet}\\[1pt]
$\bullet$ \textbf{The cooling channels are not resolved as air flow.} They are
a convective boundary condition on the channel-facing surfaces. The consequence
matters: the cell-to-cell spread this model produces comes from the two end
cells having one cooled face while the six interior cells have two. It does
\emph{not} include coolant heating along the channel --- a real effect this
model cannot see.\\
$\bullet$ The convective coefficient is calculated from the stated channel flow
($8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ in a $3\,\mathrm{mm}$ gap), not measured, and not
validated against anything.\\
$\bullet$ Material properties are representative, not measured for your cells;
conductivity is treated as equal in all directions.\\
$\bullet$ The stack ends along the flow direction are insulated; no inlet or
exit plenum is modelled.\\
$\bullet$ Cells exchange heat only through the channel faces; no direct
cell-to-cell contact path is modelled.\\
$\bullet$ The confidence check of section 1 is on conduction with convective
surfaces. It says nothing about the channel airflow.}
\end{minipage}}
\end{minipage}\hfill

\emph{Confidence check, section 1:} upfront estimate $87.902$ core-minutes;

owner rather than metered on the machine.}

\vspace{1pt}
{\footnotesize
\rolesig{Lead Researcher.} The exact-solution check comes first on purpose. The
physics your module needs --- heat generated inside a solid, conducted out,
removed by a convective surface, all changing in time --- is exactly what can be
checked against a closed-form answer, and we checked it to within
$0.013\,\%$. That is the basis on which we would ask you to believe a module
prediction. The module prediction does not exist yet and we will not sketch
one.

\rolesig{Lead Engineer.} One design point is already worth flagging, and it is
geometric rather than numerical: the two end cells are cooled on one face and
the six interior cells on two. Whatever spread this model shows, that asymmetry
will dominate it. If end-cell temperature matters to you --- on a
$60\,\mathrm{s}$ takeoff pulse it usually does --- the cheapest option to
evaluate is a channel outboard of cells 1 and 8, priced as a variant once the
baseline is solved.

\rolesig{Lead Numericist.} Section 1 is a genuine verification: three
independent readings, each inside a tolerance fixed before the run and each
using less than half of it, a numerical error bar of $0.0154\,\%$ at worst, and
a measured order of accuracy of $2.00$ against a theoretical $2.00$. Section 3
is empty because the module has not run. When it does, the first two figures we
report --- before any temperature --- are the cumulative energy closure and the
comparison between the two time steps, $0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ and
$0.25\,\mathrm{s}$, which is what tells you the answer is set by the physics and
not by the step size.\vspace{-3pt}}
