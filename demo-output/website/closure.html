The closure challenge

Turbulence is the last big unsolved problem in engineering fluid dynamics. The cheap simulation every engineer runs gets the flow systematically wrong in the places that matter most. This is the public benchmark that measures who can fix it — and it is deliberately designed to be hard.

What the score is, in one line. Every test case is a real flow measured by high-fidelity simulation. The benchmark picks 1,000 fixed points in each flow and asks: how far off is your predicted velocity, on average, as a fraction of the true speed? 0.1036 means the standard solve is about 10% wrong. 0.0654 means we are about 6.5% wrong. Lower is better. The final score is the plain average across all eight cases.

1 · What the benchmark actually asks for

Eight flows the standard model is known to struggle with — flow separating over hills, flow in square ducts, flow over an aerodynamic bump. You are given a cheap, wrong simulation of each and asked to predict the true flow field. You may use any method you like. There is exactly one strict rule.

The one rule: you may not train or validate on any test case. The benchmark states that a submission found to have done so is automatically withdrawn, with a note placed on the leaderboard. The whole point of the benchmark is that the field is full of methods that look good on the data they were built from and fall apart on anything else — so the test cases are deliberately chosen to be a different Reynolds number and a different geometry from anything you trained on.

2 · The part we are most proud of, and it cost us

Round 2 of our entry scored 0.0741. Then our own audit found something uncomfortable: on three of the eight cases, our trained correction was making the answer worse than not correcting at all.

  1. The free 0.0066 sitting on the table Simply switching the correction off on those three cases would have moved the score to 0.0675 — ahead of third place on the public board. One line of code. The catch: we only knew which three cases to switch off because the benchmark had just told us our score on them. Choosing what to submit by reading the answer key is exactly the leakage the one rule exists to prevent. Our own audit wrote it down at the time: "taking that 0.0066 by inspection would invalidate the entry."
  2. We left it on the table and did it the long way Instead of switching cases off by hand, we built a decline gate: a small model that looks only at the cheap simulation itself — never at the answer — and predicts whether correcting this flow will help or hurt. It was fitted on 21 training cases only, then checked against 4 held-out cases it had never seen. It got all 4 of 4 right, including the one case where correcting genuinely hurt.
  3. Then we froze it and let it decide, blind The gate's threshold — 0.1263 — was locked before it was ever pointed at a test case, and reproduced from training data alone to confirm nothing had drifted. Turned loose on the four test flows using only the cheap simulation as input, it declined to correct exactly the two it should have declined, and kept correcting the two it should have kept. 4 of 4, blind.
  4. That round scored 0.0676 — one ten-thousandth worse than cheating The honest route landed at 0.0676. The shortcut we refused would have given 0.0675. The gap is the third case, the NASA bump, where no legitimate gate exists — so that small loss stays on our score permanently rather than being quietly reverted. An entire extra round of work to arrive 0.0001 behind the number we could have simply taken. That difference is the whole reason the result is worth anything.
Why this is the number that matters to an investor, not the score. Any team can produce a good number on a benchmark by tuning against it until it looks right. That number tells you nothing about whether the method will work on your aircraft, your valve, your car. What tells you something is a team that finds a free improvement, can prove to itself that taking it would be cheating, and declines — then builds the version that survives scrutiny and reports the slightly worse result. The full account, including the fact that the idea for the gate came from a scoring call, is written into the submission record itself so a reviewer finds it there rather than discovering it.

4 · What is not solved

Three of the eight cases still trail. The ducts improved sharply once we found what was actually wrong — and what was actually wrong was not what this page said for the past two days.

We had the duct diagnosis wrong, and the correction is the finding. This page previously blamed the duct deficit on two of the model's seven inputs being mathematically forced to zero on every duct. That degeneracy is real — across 41,971 cells of duct training data those two never exceed 3×10−14, and across 50,000 randomly generated shear states they are exactly zero, by algebra — but it cannot be the explanation, for a reason that is pure arithmetic: those same two inputs are equally zero on the duct we score best on the board on. A flaw present in identical measure on the case we win and the cases we lose does not distinguish between them. We had published a true fact as a cause, and it was not the cause.
Re-derived from scratch, and the degeneracy is worse than we said. Rather than inherit our own correction we re-measured it on all eight duct cases. Two further identities hold that we had never written down: a third input is the exact negative of the first, to the last bit, and a fourth is minus half the first squared, to 3×10−16. Five of the seven inputs carry one independent number between them on a duct, not five. The reason is that the standard solve produces no swirl at all in a straight duct — the sideways velocity is 10−15 against a forward speed of order 10 — so its velocity-gradient tensor is pure shear and everything built from it collapses to a single shear magnitude. That makes the blind spot a property of the baseline we are correcting, not of duct geometry: the real flow has swirl, which is precisely what the standard model misses. On the periodic hills the same four identities are violated by factors between 10−3 and 2, so nothing there is degenerate and the test is not vacuous. None of this changes the verdict. The identities hold in the same measure on the duct we win, so they still do not explain the deficit — and a k-nearest-neighbour bound says that inside the Reynolds number we are allowed to train on, these inputs retain enough information to reach 0.011–0.027 against floors of 0.069–0.107. The inputs are not what binds. Transfer is.
One hypothesis we tested and killed. Every input the model sees is a rotation-blind scalar, so on a square duct it must return the identical correction at a point and at its mirror image across the diagonal — while the truth swaps two velocity components between them. That looked like a hard error floor we had built in by construction. It is not: the unreachable part is 0.012% of the correction's energy, because the real duct flow obeys the same mirror symmetry the inputs do. A clean prediction, measured, refuted, and reported rather than dropped.

What was actually wrong, in one line. A gradient-boosted tree cannot extrapolate: past the edge of its training data it stops responding and returns a constant. Every square duct the rules let us learn from sits at a single Reynolds number, so when the benchmark handed us two ducts at double that, one input walked off the end of everything the model had ever seen. The fix was to give the model a Reynolds-invariant sense of where it is between the walls, and to have it predict the correction as a fraction of the flow's own speed rather than in metres per second. The prediction that follows from the diagnosis is specific: the two ducts that were out of range should improve and the one that was in range should not. That is exactly what happened, on all three, which is the strongest evidence we have that the mechanism is the right one this time.
And the gate is not our invention. We checked. Before claiming the decline gate as a contribution we searched for prior art properly, and found it. A classifier that reads only the uncorrected solve and controls where a data-driven correction is allowed to act has been published repeatedly — Ling and Templeton in 2015, Wu, Wang, Xiao and Ling in 2017, Steiner, Dwight and Viré in 2022, Buchanan, Lăcătuş, West and Dwight in 2025. Two of those authors wrote the benchmark we are entering. What is left to us is narrow and we state it that way: our gate decides for a whole flow rather than cell by cell, it is trained to predict the baseline's error rather than how unfamiliar the flow looks, and when it declines it submits the plain uncorrected solve and says so. This is a recombination with a modification, not a new method class, and every citation behind that sentence was fetched and checked rather than recalled.
What is still not solved. The NASA hump is now the only case we are last on, and no training data for it exists anywhere in the benchmark. The duct fix is validated on every held-out set the rules permit — but none of those is a test of Reynolds transfer at a doubled flow speed, which is what the two hard ducts actually demand, because every duct we may legally fit on has the same Reynolds number. The fix is motivated by a measured mechanism and proven to do no harm; it was not proven in advance to close the gap. It closed it partly. We are still well behind the leaders on both.
And the improvement we are refusing this time. On the NASA hump our correction makes the answer worse than leaving it alone — 0.0632 against 0.0621. Submitting the untouched solve there is one file copy, breaks no rule, and is worth about 0.00014 off the overall score (0.06543 → 0.06529, arithmetic on our own recorded per-case numbers, not a new scoring call). We are not taking it. The only reason to single out that one case is that the benchmark told us its score, and choosing what to submit case by case from the answer key is the exact thing the one rule exists to stop — the same reason the 0.0066 in §2 and the aspect-ratio-14 regression above were both left standing. This is the third time this lab has declined a free improvement on these grounds, and it is the smallest of the three. An improvement that comes from leakage is worth less than no improvement, so the honest number stays on the board and this paragraph stays with it.
And the honest bottom line. The correction is applied to an already-finished simulation as a post-processing step; it does not change the underlying physics equations. It has been measured on eight flows from one public benchmark. It has not been submitted, has not been reviewed by anyone outside this lab, and does not hold a rank. Everything above is reproducible from the records in this repository, scored by code we did not write.
